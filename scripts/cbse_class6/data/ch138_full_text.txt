CHAPTER 4 — TIMELINE AND SOURCES OF HISTORY

INTRODUCTION

History is the study of the past. But the past is enormous: it stretches back tens of thousands of years and contains billions of human lives. To make sense of it, historians do two things. First, they place events on a TIMELINE so they can see what came before and what came after. Second, they read SOURCES — old objects, writings and stories that survived from earlier times — and ask careful questions of those sources. This chapter teaches both: how time is measured in history, and how we know what we know about the past.

4.1 THE IDEA OF A TIMELINE

A timeline is a line on which events are placed in the order they happened. The line usually runs left-to-right, with earlier events on the left and later events on the right. The distance between two events on a timeline matches how far apart they were in real time. If 100 years separates two events, they appear ten times further apart than two events separated by 10 years.

Timelines help us in three ways. They show ORDER (which event came first). They show DURATION (how long something lasted, like the reign of a king or a dynasty). And they show SIMULTANEITY — what was happening in one place while something else was happening elsewhere. For example, while pyramids were being built in Egypt, the Indus or Sarasvatī cities were also flourishing in the Indian subcontinent. A timeline lets you see both at once.

4.2 HOW TIME IS COUNTED — BCE AND CE

Historians count years using a fixed reference point. In the calendar most widely used today, year 1 CE (Common Era) is the reference. Years AFTER it are called CE; years BEFORE it are called BCE (Before Common Era). So 500 CE is 500 years after the reference, and 500 BCE is 500 years before. The older labels AD ('Anno Domini') and BC ('Before Christ') mean the same as CE and BCE; modern textbooks prefer CE/BCE because they are religion-neutral.

Two important rules about BCE numbers:
• Bigger BCE numbers are EARLIER. 3000 BCE is older than 2000 BCE.
• There is NO YEAR ZERO. Going back from 1 CE you immediately reach 1 BCE.

To compute the gap between a BCE date and a CE date, ADD them and subtract 1. From 100 BCE to 100 CE is 100 + 100 − 1 = 199 years. To compute the gap between two BCE dates, SUBTRACT (the older from the more recent). From 3000 BCE to 1500 BCE is 1500 years.

Some Indian texts also use the Vikram Samvat (start: 57 BCE) and Shaka Samvat (start: 78 CE) eras. To convert: a Vikram Samvat year minus 57 ≈ CE year (if positive). A Shaka Samvat year plus 78 = CE year.

4.3 BIG BLOCKS OF TIME

Historians group years into bigger blocks so they are easier to talk about:
• A DECADE = 10 years.
• A CENTURY = 100 years. The 21st century runs 2001–2100 CE.
• A MILLENNIUM = 1000 years. We are currently in the 3rd millennium CE.

For very long stretches before written records, historians use even bigger labels: prehistory (before writing), and within prehistory the Stone Age, Bronze Age and Iron Age, named after the main material used for tools.

4.4 THREE BROAD PERIODS OF INDIAN HISTORY

Historians often divide Indian history into three broad periods. These are convenient labels, not sharp walls:
• ANCIENT — from earliest human settlements through about 700 CE. Includes the Harappan civilisation, the Vedic period, the rise of Magadha, the Mauryas and the Guptas.
• MEDIEVAL — roughly 700 CE to 1700 CE. Includes the rise of regional kingdoms, the Cholas, the Delhi Sultanate, Vijayanagara, the Mughals and the Marathas.
• MODERN — from about 1700 CE to today. Includes British rule, the freedom struggle, Independence in 1947, and the Republic from 1950.

These boundaries are blurry — change is gradual, and life in 700 CE looked a lot like life in 650 CE. The labels just make it easier to organise a long story.

4.5 SOURCES — HOW HISTORIANS KNOW THE PAST

Everything we know about the past comes from SOURCES — surviving evidence. Sources are usually grouped into three families:

• ARCHAEOLOGICAL sources — physical objects that survived: pottery, tools, coins, beads, jewellery, weapons, ruins of houses and forts, seals, bones, even seeds. Archaeologists dig these out of the ground (excavation) and study them in laboratories.
• LITERARY (written) sources — texts written long ago: the Vedas, the Mahābhārata and Rāmāyaṇa, Buddhist Tripitakas, Jaina Āgamas, Sangam poetry, royal decrees on inscriptions, foreign travellers' diaries, palm-leaf manuscripts, copper plates.
• ORAL sources — songs, stories, family histories, proverbs and traditions passed down by word of mouth across generations.

Each kind of source is partial. Archaeology shows objects but rarely WHO used them. Texts give names and ideas but were often written by a small literate elite. Oral traditions remember big events but blur the dates. So a careful historian uses MANY sources together — like crime detectives gathering several pieces of evidence — and cross-checks them.

4.6 ARCHAEOLOGY UP CLOSE

Archaeologists look not just at single objects, but at the LAYERS in which they are buried. Older layers lie deeper, newer layers lie above. So if Pot A is found below Pot B at the same site, A is older than B. This idea is called STRATIGRAPHY ('study of strata' or layers).

To put real numbers on the dates, archaeologists use scientific tests. RADIOCARBON DATING (also called C-14 dating) measures how much of a special type of carbon is left in once-living material such as wood, charcoal, bone or seeds. The older the sample, the less C-14 remains. The test gives an age in years (with a small uncertainty).

Important Indian archaeological sites include Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, Dholavira and Lothal (Harappan civilisation); Pātaliputra (modern Patna, capital of the Mauryas); Sānchī (Buddhist stupa); Hampi (Vijayanagara) and the rock-cut caves of Ajanta, Ellora and Mahabalipuram. Each site, dug carefully, adds details to the bigger picture.

4.7 LITERARY SOURCES UP CLOSE

Texts add what objects cannot — names of kings, ideas about gods, laws, poetry. The earliest Indian texts are the four VEDAS (Ṛig, Sāma, Yajur, Atharva), composed in Sanskrit and remembered orally for many generations before being written down. The two great epics are the MAHĀBHĀRATA and RĀMĀYAṆA. Buddhists preserved the TRIPIṬAKAS in Pāli; Jainas preserved their ĀGAMAS in Prakrit. The SANGAM literature in Tamil gives a vivid picture of South India in early centuries CE.

INSCRIPTIONS are short texts carved on stone, copper plates or pillars. They are very useful: they were carved at the time of the events and they survive well. Emperor ASHOKA's edicts, carved on rocks and pillars across the subcontinent in the 3rd century BCE, are one of the most famous sets of inscriptions in world history. They tell us about Ashoka's policies, his concern for his subjects, and his use of moral law (dhamma).

COINS are another excellent source — they show kings' names, their portraits, the languages used, and their religious symbols. A coin found far from where it was minted tells us about trade.

4.8 ORAL SOURCES UP CLOSE

Long before writing, people remembered their past in songs, stories and chants. Many bardic traditions across India still preserve genealogies of royal families and accounts of battles. The folk epics of Pabuji in Rajasthan, the songs of Kannaki in Tamil tradition, family kuladevatā stories — all are oral sources. They may shift small details over time, but the major characters and themes survive remarkably faithfully across centuries.

Modern historians treat oral histories carefully. They record them, compare different versions, and check them against archaeology and texts.

4.9 ASKING QUESTIONS OF A SOURCE

A source by itself is silent. The historian's job is to ASK QUESTIONS:
• WHO made this source — a king, a priest, a traveller, a craftsperson?
• WHEN was it made? Can we date it directly, or only by what surrounds it?
• WHY was it made? Inscriptions celebrate a king; coins serve trade; epics teach ideals.
• WHAT does it leave OUT? A royal inscription rarely mentions the king's failures.
• Does another source AGREE? If two independent sources say the same thing, that's strong evidence.

Good history asks all these questions before drawing conclusions.

SUMMARY

A timeline orders events in time and shows how long they lasted. Years are counted as BCE (older = bigger number) and CE (newer = bigger number); there is no year zero. Indian history is broadly grouped into Ancient (up to ~700 CE), Medieval (~700–1700 CE) and Modern (~1700 CE to today). Sources for studying the past are archaeological (objects), literary (texts and inscriptions) and oral (songs, stories). Stratigraphy and radiocarbon dating help date archaeological finds. Inscriptions, coins and ancient texts like the Vedas, epics, Tripiṭakas and Sangam poetry add the human voice. A historian uses many sources together and asks careful questions of each.
