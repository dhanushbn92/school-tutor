CHAPTER 1 — SCHOOL KITCHEN GARDEN

INTRODUCTION

Have you ever pulled a tomato off a vine and eaten it the same day? Or watched a tiny seed turn into a coriander plant tall enough to add to your sister's chutney? A kitchen garden makes both of these possible — right at school. In this chapter you will learn what a kitchen garden is, how to plan one, what tools and materials you need, and how to sow, care for and harvest plants. You will also learn safe practices, simple observations to record, and how to share what you grow.

A kitchen garden is a small piece of land — or a few pots — used to grow food crops: vegetables, herbs and sometimes fruits. Schools across India have set up kitchen gardens to teach students where food comes from, how plants grow, and how to work together on a long, real project.

1.1 WHAT IS A KITCHEN GARDEN?

A kitchen garden is a small garden where you grow plants you can eat. It is different from a flower garden (where you grow plants for their beauty) and a forest (where plants grow without human care). A kitchen garden is small, useful, and tended every day.

Why have a kitchen garden at school?
• Food you grow yourself is fresh, safe and full of nutrients.
• You learn the skills of planning, observation, patience and care.
• A kitchen garden invites bees and butterflies — good for pollination.
• It helps the school become greener and cooler.
• Sharing the harvest builds friendship.

A kitchen garden can be as small as five pots on a balcony or as large as a school field. The principle is the same: plants need soil, water, sunlight, air and care.

1.2 PLANNING THE GARDEN

Before you put a single seed in the ground, plan! Good planning saves work later.

Choose a spot. Pick a place that gets at least four to six hours of sunlight a day. The spot should be away from heavy footfall and easy to water. Plant beds in the school field, raised beds, pots on a verandah, or even bottle-gardens on a wall — all work.

Decide what to grow. For a Class 6 garden, easy first crops are: tomato, brinjal (baingan), chilli, lady's finger (bhindi), spinach (palak), coriander (dhania), mint (pudina), curry leaves, and small herbs like tulsi. Try to mix tall, medium and short plants so each gets sunlight.

Decide WHEN to grow. India has three main growing seasons:
• Kharif — sown in June/July with the monsoon, harvested in September/October. Examples: gourds, chillies, brinjals, beans.
• Rabi — sown in October/November, harvested in February/March. Examples: spinach, coriander, peas, carrot, radish.
• Zaid — sown in March/April, harvested in May/June. Examples: cucumber, watermelon, summer squash.

Draw a simple plan. On paper, sketch the bed and mark where each plant will go. Leave room between plants — about a hand's-width for small herbs, a forearm's-length for big plants like brinjal.

1.3 TOOLS AND MATERIALS

A kitchen garden uses simple, low-cost tools. Always use them carefully and put them back after use.

Tools:
• Khurpi (small hand hoe) — for digging and weeding.
• Trowel — for transplanting seedlings.
• Watering can or a clean bottle with a sprinkler cap.
• A rake — to level soil.
• Gloves — to protect hands.

Materials:
• Soil and compost (mix two parts soil to one part compost).
• Seeds — use good-quality seeds from a trusted source.
• Pots, plant beds or recycled containers (clean and with drainage holes).
• Mulch (dry leaves, hay, coconut fibre) — to keep soil moist.
• Stakes or thin sticks — to support tall plants.

Safety: never put your hands in the soil without checking first; insects, sharp stones or thorns can cause cuts. Wash hands with soap after gardening. Use sun caps and water bottles for yourself when you work outdoors.

1.4 SOWING AND GROWING

Three ways to start a plant:
• Direct sowing — put the seed straight into the bed (e.g. spinach, coriander, radish).
• Seedlings — start seeds in a small tray, then transplant the small plants into the bed (e.g. tomato, brinjal, chilli).
• Cuttings — take a piece of stem of a plant like mint or curry leaves and put it in moist soil; it grows roots.

Steps for direct sowing:
1. Loosen the soil to about a forearm's depth.
2. Mix in compost.
3. Make small holes about two finger-widths deep.
4. Drop one or two seeds in each hole.
5. Cover lightly with soil.
6. Water gently — a fine spray, not a flood.
7. Mark each row with a small wooden tag so you know what was planted where.

What seeds need to germinate (sprout): moisture, warmth and time. Different seeds take different days — radish in 3–5 days, coriander in 7–10, tomato in 7–14.

1.5 CARING FOR PLANTS

A garden is not 'finished' the day you plant the seeds. It needs everyday care.

Water carefully. Most kitchen-garden plants need water every one or two days, depending on the weather. Best time: early morning or late afternoon. Don't water at noon — water can scorch leaves. Touch the soil before watering: if it is still moist, skip a day.

Weed regularly. Weeds are unwanted plants that take water, nutrients and light away from your crops. Pull them out by their roots once a week.

Mulch. A layer of dry leaves on top of the soil keeps it moist, cool and weed-free.

Watch for pests. Caterpillars, aphids and snails sometimes eat leaves. For a school garden, use safe methods: pick caterpillars off by hand, spray a mild neem-water solution, or plant marigolds nearby — they keep some pests away.

Stake tall plants. Tomato and brinjal grow heavy. Tie them gently to a thin stick so they do not fall.

Keep a garden diary. Each week, record: date, weather, what you saw (new flower? insect?), what you did. The diary helps you learn from one season to the next.

1.6 HARVESTING AND USING THE PRODUCE

Harvest is the moment your work pays off!
• Spinach and coriander — pick leaves once they are large; the plant keeps growing.
• Radish, carrot — pull the whole plant when the root is firm.
• Tomato, brinjal, chilli — pluck when ripe; check colour and firmness.
• Mint, curry leaves — snip a few leaves at a time; never strip the whole plant.

Wash everything well. Share with the school cook for the midday meal, or take home a small bag with permission. Save some seeds from the best plants for next season — that's how farmers have multiplied seeds for thousands of years.

Reflection. After each project, ask yourself: which plants thrived? which struggled? what would I do differently? Reflection turns experience into skill.

SUMMARY

A kitchen garden is a small useful garden where you grow plants you can eat. Choose a sunny spot, plan what to grow with the seasons (Kharif, Rabi, Zaid), and draw a simple plan. Use simple tools — khurpi, trowel, watering can — and work safely. Start plants by direct sowing, seedlings or cuttings. Care for plants by watering early, weeding weekly, mulching, watching for pests, staking tall plants and keeping a diary. Harvest carefully, wash, share, and save seeds. A kitchen garden teaches you skills of planning, observation, patience and teamwork — and gives you fresh food in return.
