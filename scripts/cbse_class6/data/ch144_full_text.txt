CHAPTER 10 — GRASSROOTS DEMOCRACY (PART 1): GOVERNANCE

INTRODUCTION

Every day we make small decisions for ourselves — what to wear, what to eat, when to study. But many decisions in life cannot be taken alone. Should there be a road through the village? How should clean water be supplied to a city? Who decides whether a forest is cut down or saved? These are decisions about MANY people, made BY people, for the GOOD of all. The art of taking such decisions together — fairly, peacefully and in everyone's interest — is called GOVERNANCE. This chapter introduces governance and the special form of governance called DEMOCRACY, in which the people themselves are the rulers.

10.1 WHAT IS GOVERNANCE?

Governance is the way a community organises itself to take decisions and get things done. The word comes from a Greek root meaning 'to steer' — like steering a boat. Whenever a group lives together, somebody must decide on the common rules and somebody must see that the rules are followed.

Governance happens at many scales. A family decides what to cook for dinner. A school decides what time classes start. A village decides where to build a well. A country decides whether to go to war or sign a treaty. The scale is different but the question is the same: WHO decides, HOW, and FOR WHOM?

Three things make good governance work: clear RULES (so everyone knows what to expect), fair PROCESSES (so the rules are applied to all without favouritism), and ACCOUNTABILITY (so leaders can be questioned if things go wrong). When these three things are missing, even small problems become big.

10.2 THREE BIG QUESTIONS OF GOVERNANCE

Across history, communities have answered three big questions in many different ways:

• WHO RULES? One person, or many? A king, a council, a chief, a parliament, the whole people?
• HOW ARE DECISIONS MADE? By orders from the top? By long discussion until everyone agrees? By a vote?
• HOW IS POWER LIMITED? Are there laws even the ruler must obey? Who can challenge the ruler?

Different societies have given different answers. In ancient kingdoms, often one king ruled and his word was law. In ancient republics like the Vajji confederacy in north India (around the time of the Buddha), a council of elders met and took decisions by discussion. In modern India, the people themselves elect their rulers and the Constitution limits everyone's power.

10.3 WHAT IS DEMOCRACY?

DEMOCRACY is a form of governance in which the PEOPLE are the rulers. The word comes from two Greek words: demos (the people) and kratos (rule or power). 'Power of the people'.

Democracy does not mean every person votes on every single decision — that would be impossible in a country of more than 140 crore people. In a REPRESENTATIVE democracy (which India and most modern democracies are), the people elect REPRESENTATIVES who then take decisions on their behalf. If those representatives do a poor job, the people can vote them out at the next election.

Five features make a democracy:
• People CHOOSE their rulers through fair elections.
• Every adult citizen has ONE vote of equal value.
• Different parties and candidates can compete; no single party is forced on the people.
• The ruler's power is LIMITED by laws and a Constitution.
• Citizens have RIGHTS that the ruler cannot take away — speech, faith, fair trial.

10.4 INDIA AS THE WORLD'S LARGEST DEMOCRACY

India became independent on 15 August 1947. The Constitution of India came into effect on 26 January 1950 — that is why we celebrate Republic Day on 26 January. Dr B. R. Ambedkar chaired the Drafting Committee that prepared the Constitution. The Constitution turned India into a SOVEREIGN, SOCIALIST, SECULAR, DEMOCRATIC, REPUBLIC.

Five big words — what they mean simply:
• SOVEREIGN — independent; no foreign country tells India what to do.
• SOCIALIST — the country aims to reduce inequality so the poor are not left behind.
• SECULAR — the state has no official religion; all religions are treated equally.
• DEMOCRATIC — the people choose their government.
• REPUBLIC — the head of state (the President) is elected, not a hereditary king.

India is the world's LARGEST democracy by population: hundreds of millions of voters take part in every general election, in dozens of languages, across the whole subcontinent.

10.5 LEVELS OF GOVERNMENT IN INDIA

Indian governance has THREE levels, like a three-storey building:

• UNION (CENTRAL) GOVERNMENT — at the national level. It deals with subjects that affect the whole country: defence, foreign affairs, currency, railways, post.
• STATE GOVERNMENT — one for each state and Union Territory with a legislature. It deals with subjects within the state: police, agriculture, public health, education to a large extent.
• LOCAL GOVERNMENT — at the village or city level. This is the level closest to ordinary people and decides on local matters: village wells, street lights, school maintenance, local sanitation.

Local government in rural areas is called the PANCHAYATI RAJ system; in urban areas it includes the Municipal Corporation, the Municipal Council and the Nagar Panchayat. The next two chapters will go into detail. This chapter introduces the IDEA.

10.6 WHY LOCAL GOVERNMENT MATTERS

The Union and State governments are far away from a particular village or neighbourhood. They cannot know whether the well in YOUR street is broken, or whether the school in YOUR village needs another teacher. Local government exists so that local problems can be solved by people who actually live with them.

A famous Indian principle for local self-government is GRAM SWARAJ — village self-rule — championed by Mahatma Gandhi. He argued that a strong India must have strong villages, where the people make decisions for themselves and where every voice — including the voices of women, the very poor and the marginalised — is heard.

In 1992, the 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments gave a strong constitutional foundation to local government. They required regular elections, reserved seats for women and for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, and gave Panchayats and Municipalities a clear list of powers. These amendments turned local government from an idea into a working reality across India.

10.7 RIGHTS, DUTIES AND PARTICIPATION

A democracy gives people RIGHTS — but it also expects DUTIES. The Constitution lists FUNDAMENTAL RIGHTS such as the right to equality, to freedom of speech and expression, to practise one's religion, and to be protected by fair laws. It also lists FUNDAMENTAL DUTIES such as respecting the Constitution, protecting public property, valuing the rich heritage of our composite culture, and safeguarding the environment.

PARTICIPATION is the heart of democracy. A citizen of India can:
• VOTE in elections from age 18.
• Stand for election to a Panchayat, Municipality, State Assembly or Parliament (with age and other rules).
• Contact representatives — write to them, attend their public meetings, raise issues at gram sabha or ward meetings.
• Form associations and peacefully campaign for causes.
• Use the right to information (RTI) to ask the government for facts and figures.

Children too are part of governance — through school councils, by speaking up at family or class meetings, and by writing to local newspapers. Practising democracy at school is a way of preparing for democracy at the larger scale.

10.8 GOVERNANCE BEYOND ELECTIONS

Voting once every five years is necessary but not sufficient. Good governance also depends on:
• A FREE PRESS — newspapers and TV that can criticise the government without fear.
• An INDEPENDENT JUDICIARY — courts that can strike down bad laws.
• STRONG INSTITUTIONS — Election Commission, Comptroller and Auditor General, public service commissions — that work without political interference.
• CIVIL SOCIETY — non-government organisations, citizens' groups, gram sabhas — that hold the government accountable between elections.

When these are weak, even an elected government can act badly. When they are strong, even a popular government has to listen to its people.

SUMMARY

Governance is the way a community takes decisions and gets things done. Across history, societies have answered three big questions — WHO rules, HOW decisions are made, HOW power is limited — in many ways. Democracy is the form of governance where the people themselves rule, usually through elected representatives. India is the world's largest democracy, founded by the Constitution that came into effect on 26 January 1950 — sovereign, socialist, secular, democratic, republic. India has three levels of government: Union, State and Local. Local government — Panchayati Raj in villages and Municipalities in cities — was given strong constitutional backing by the 73rd and 74th Amendments of 1992. Citizens have rights AND duties; participation is the heart of democracy. Beyond elections, free press, independent courts, strong institutions and active civil society keep governance honest.
