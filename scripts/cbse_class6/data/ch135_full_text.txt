CHAPTER 1 — LOCATING PLACES ON THE EARTH

Introduction

Where in the world is your school? Where is the city of Mumbai? Where is the South Pole? To answer questions like these we need a SYSTEM — a set of imaginary lines drawn on the Earth that lets us point to any place precisely. This chapter shows you that system: latitudes, longitudes, the major reference lines, and how to read a coordinate to find a place.


1.1 THE SHAPE OF THE EARTH

The Earth is a sphere — almost, but not quite. It is slightly flattened at the two poles and slightly bulged at the EQUATOR. This shape is called an OBLATE SPHEROID. From far away in space, however, the Earth looks like a perfect blue-and-green ball.

The two ENDPOINTS of the Earth's spinning axis are called the GEOGRAPHIC POLES — the NORTH POLE (top) and the SOUTH POLE (bottom). The Earth turns once around this axis in about 24 hours, giving us day and night.


1.2 GLOBES AND MAPS

A GLOBE is a small round model of the Earth. Because it is round like the Earth, a globe is the most accurate way to show shapes, sizes and positions of countries and oceans. Its drawback: it cannot show details of small areas, and it is not easy to carry.

A MAP is a flat drawing of the Earth (or part of it). Maps are easy to carry and can show small details. Their drawback: because the Earth is round and a map is flat, every map distorts (stretches) shapes or sizes a little.

Globe and map work together: the globe is for the whole picture; the map is for detail.


1.3 LATITUDES — IMAGINARY EAST-WEST LINES

To locate a place, we need a grid of lines. The first set of lines run EAST-WEST around the Earth, parallel to the equator. They are called LINES OF LATITUDE or PARALLELS.

- The EQUATOR is the most important parallel. It is 0° latitude. It divides the Earth into two equal halves: the NORTHERN HEMISPHERE (above the equator) and the SOUTHERN HEMISPHERE (below).
- Latitudes are measured in DEGREES — from 0° at the equator to 90° at each pole.
- The North Pole is 90° N (north). The South Pole is 90° S (south).

Important named parallels.
- TROPIC OF CANCER: 23½° N (about 23.5° north). It passes through India.
- TROPIC OF CAPRICORN: 23½° S.
- ARCTIC CIRCLE: 66½° N.
- ANTARCTIC CIRCLE: 66½° S.

The region between the two tropics is called the TORRID ZONE — it is the hottest part of the Earth (the sun is overhead twice a year). The regions between the tropics and the polar circles are the TEMPERATE ZONES (mild). The regions inside the polar circles are the FRIGID ZONES (very cold).


1.4 LONGITUDES — IMAGINARY NORTH-SOUTH LINES

The second set of lines run from the NORTH POLE to the SOUTH POLE. They are called LINES OF LONGITUDE or MERIDIANS.

- The PRIME MERIDIAN is the most important meridian. It is 0° longitude. It passes through Greenwich (near London).
- Longitudes are measured in degrees — from 0° at the Prime Meridian to 180° east AND 180° west.
- East of the Prime Meridian: 1° E, 2° E, … 180° E.
- West of the Prime Meridian: 1° W, 2° W, … 180° W.
- 180° E and 180° W are the same meridian — called the INTERNATIONAL DATE LINE.

Unlike parallels (which become smaller circles closer to the poles), all meridians are the same length — each one runs from pole to pole.

Note. The Prime Meridian and the equator together divide the Earth into FOUR pieces: the four quadrants.


1.5 THE GEOGRAPHIC GRID — LOCATING A PLACE

The geographic grid is the network formed by latitudes and longitudes. Every place on Earth has a unique COORDINATE — a (latitude, longitude) pair.

How to read a coordinate.
- Latitude first, then longitude.
- Use N or S for latitude (above / below equator); E or W for longitude (east / west of Prime Meridian).
- Example: New Delhi is approximately at (28.6° N, 77.2° E). That means 28.6° north of the equator AND 77.2° east of the Prime Meridian.
- Example: Mumbai is approximately at (19.1° N, 72.9° E).
- Example: Sydney (Australia) is approximately at (33.9° S, 151.2° E).

To find a place from coordinates:
1. Find the latitude line (north or south of the equator).
2. Find the longitude line (east or west of the Prime Meridian).
3. The point where they cross is the place.

To get coordinates of a known place:
1. Drop a horizontal (east-west) line through the place to read its latitude.
2. Drop a vertical (north-south) line through the place to read its longitude.


1.6 GREAT CIRCLES AND TIME ZONES

A GREAT CIRCLE is the largest possible circle that can be drawn on a sphere — its centre is the centre of the Earth. The EQUATOR is a great circle. Each MERIDIAN paired with its opposite meridian (e.g., 0° and 180°) forms a great circle. Great circles divide the Earth into two equal halves.

A SMALL CIRCLE is any circle on the Earth that is NOT a great circle. All parallels OTHER than the equator are small circles.

Standard time and time zones (briefly). The Earth turns 360° in 24 hours, so it turns 15° in 1 hour. Each 15° of longitude corresponds to 1 hour of time difference. India uses INDIAN STANDARD TIME (IST), which is set to 82.5° E (passing through Mirzapur). When it is 12 noon at the Prime Meridian (Greenwich), it is 5:30 pm in India.


SUMMARY

- The Earth is an oblate spheroid; the spinning axis ends at the two poles.
- Globes are round and accurate; maps are flat and convenient — both are useful.
- LATITUDES (east-west lines, parallels) are measured 0°-90° N or S; equator = 0°.
- Important parallels: Tropic of Cancer (23½° N), Tropic of Capricorn (23½° S), Arctic Circle (66½° N), Antarctic Circle (66½° S), poles (90°).
- LONGITUDES (north-south lines, meridians) are measured 0°-180° E or W; Prime Meridian = 0°.
- A place's coordinate is (latitude, longitude), e.g. New Delhi is (28.6° N, 77.2° E).
- Great circles divide the Earth into halves; the equator and any meridian-pair are great circles.
- Earth rotates 15° per hour; this is the basis of time zones.
