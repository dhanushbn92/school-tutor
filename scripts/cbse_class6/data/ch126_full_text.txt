CHAPTER 2 — LINES AND ANGLES

Introduction

Geometry is the part of mathematics that studies SHAPES, LINES and SPACES. It begins with the simplest objects of all: a POINT (a position with no size), a LINE (which extends without end in both directions), and the various ways two lines can meet. From these tiny ideas we build everything — squares, triangles, buildings, maps. This chapter introduces the basic vocabulary of lines and angles and teaches you to MEASURE and CLASSIFY angles using a protractor.


2.1 POINT, LINE, LINE SEGMENT, RAY

A POINT is a position. It has no length, no width — just a location. We mark a point with a dot and a capital letter, e.g. •A.

A LINE extends without end in BOTH directions. It is straight and has no thickness. We name a line by any two points on it: line AB. The arrows on either end remind us it goes on forever.

A LINE SEGMENT is a piece of a line with TWO endpoints. It has a definite length. The line segment from P to Q is written PQ.

A RAY is a piece of a line that has ONE endpoint and extends without end in the OTHER direction — like a torch beam. We name it starting from the endpoint: ray PR (starts at P, goes through R, continues forever past R).

Quick comparison.
- Line: 0 endpoints, infinite length.
- Ray: 1 endpoint, infinite length.
- Line segment: 2 endpoints, finite length.
- Point: 0 length, just a position.


2.2 ANGLES — DEFINITION AND NAMING

An ANGLE is formed when two rays share the same endpoint. The shared endpoint is called the VERTEX, and the two rays are called the ARMS (or sides) of the angle.

Naming an angle. We use the symbol ∠ (read "angle") followed by three letters: the first and last letters are points on the two arms; the middle letter is always the vertex. So ∠ABC means the angle whose vertex is B, with one arm passing through A and the other through C. The vertex is always the middle letter.

If only one angle has its vertex at a point, we may name it just by the vertex: ∠B.

Interior of an angle. The region enclosed by the two arms is called the INTERIOR. Points inside this region are inside the angle; points outside are not.


2.3 MEASURING ANGLES — THE PROTRACTOR

We measure how 'wide' an angle is in DEGREES (°). One full turn around a point is 360°. Half a turn is 180°. A quarter turn is 90°.

A PROTRACTOR is a half-circle tool with degree markings from 0° to 180°. To measure an angle:
1. Place the centre point of the protractor exactly on the vertex.
2. Align the 0° line of the protractor with one arm of the angle.
3. Read the degree mark where the OTHER arm crosses the protractor.
4. Use the inner or outer scale, whichever starts at 0° on your aligned arm.

To draw an angle of a given measure (say 60°):
1. Draw one arm and mark its endpoint (the vertex).
2. Place the protractor's centre on the vertex; align the 0° with the arm.
3. Mark a small dot at 60°.
4. Draw a ray from the vertex through the dot — that's the second arm.

The size of the angle does NOT depend on the lengths of the arms. Two angles with the same opening but different arm lengths are still EQUAL.


2.4 TYPES OF ANGLES

We classify angles by their measure.

- ZERO angle: 0°. The two arms point in the same direction.
- ACUTE angle: greater than 0° and less than 90°. Examples: 30°, 45°, 60°, 89°.
- RIGHT angle: exactly 90°. The arms are perpendicular. We mark a right angle with a small square at the vertex.
- OBTUSE angle: greater than 90° and less than 180°. Examples: 100°, 135°, 150°.
- STRAIGHT angle: exactly 180°. The two arms point in opposite directions, forming a straight line.
- REFLEX angle: greater than 180° and less than 360°. The 'big' angle on the outside.
- COMPLETE angle: 360°. A full turn back to the start.

Memory aid:
- Acute = "small / sharp" (think: a needle).
- Obtuse = "blunt / wide" (think: an open door).
- Reflex = "more than half a turn".


2.5 PARALLEL AND INTERSECTING LINES

Two lines in the same flat surface either MEET or they don't.

PARALLEL LINES. Two lines that never meet, no matter how far you extend them, are PARALLEL. The distance between them is always the same. Symbol: AB ∥ CD ("AB is parallel to CD"). Real-world examples: the two rails of a railway track, opposite edges of a ruler.

INTERSECTING LINES. Two lines that meet (cross) at exactly one point are INTERSECTING. The point where they cross is their POINT OF INTERSECTION.

PERPENDICULAR LINES. Two intersecting lines that meet at a RIGHT ANGLE (90°) are PERPENDICULAR. Symbol: AB ⊥ CD. The corners of a book or notebook are perpendicular.


2.6 ANGLES FORMED BY INTERSECTING LINES

When two straight lines cross, they make FOUR angles around the point of intersection.

Important facts:
- Two angles SHARING an arm and lying on a straight line together add up to 180°. They are called a LINEAR PAIR.
- The two angles OPPOSITE each other (across the crossing) are EQUAL. They are called VERTICALLY OPPOSITE ANGLES.

So if one of the four angles is 70°, the angle next to it (linear pair) is 180° − 70° = 110°. The angle opposite the 70° angle is also 70°. The angle opposite the 110° angle is also 110°.

These two simple ideas — linear pair = 180° and vertically opposite = equal — let you find every angle at an intersection from just one of them.


SUMMARY

- Point, line, line segment, ray are the basic objects of geometry.
- An angle is formed by two rays sharing a common endpoint (the vertex).
- Angles are measured in degrees with a protractor; one full turn = 360°.
- Types: zero (0°), acute (<90°), right (=90°), obtuse (90°–180°), straight (180°), reflex (180°–360°), complete (360°).
- Two lines are parallel if they never meet, intersecting if they cross at one point, perpendicular if they intersect at a right angle.
- At an intersection of two straight lines: linear pairs add to 180°, vertically opposite angles are equal.
