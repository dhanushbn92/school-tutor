CHAPTER 2 — THE RAVEN AND THE FOX

Theme of the unit: Fables and Folk Tales (continued)

Introduction

Aesop, an ancient Greek storyteller, wrote some of the most famous fables in the world. His stories are short, the characters are usually animals, and the lessons are sharp. "The Raven and the Fox" is one of his most loved fables. As you read, watch how the clever fox uses words to trick a vain raven — and notice the moral hidden in those few lines.


THE FABLE (poem version)

A raven once stole a piece of cheese
And flew to a tall tree to eat it at ease.
Below sat a hungry, sly old fox,
Who looked up smiling, calm as the rocks.

"How beautiful you are!" said Fox to the raven.
"Your shining black feathers, your wings so heaven-
ly! And surely the bird with such grace and air
Must have a voice that is also fair?"

The raven's heart swelled — he could not refuse.
He opened his beak to give Fox his news,
And out fell the cheese with a soft little PLOP.
The fox snatched it up — and away he hopped!

"My dear raven friend," said the fox with a grin,
"You have voice enough — but you have no SENSE within.
Beware of those who flatter and praise:
They speak sweet words but seek their own ways."


THE STORY (in prose, simply)

A raven sits high on the branch of a tree, holding a piece of cheese in his beak. A hungry fox comes and sees the cheese. The fox knows he cannot climb up to the raven, so he must use a TRICK.

The fox says, "What a beautiful raven you are! Your feathers are shining and black. Your wings are like the wings of an eagle." The raven listens; he is pleased.

The fox continues, "But surely, with such beauty, you must have a wonderful voice too! If only I could hear you sing!"

The raven, full of pride, opens his beak to caw — and the cheese falls to the ground. The fox quickly grabs the cheese and runs away.

Before going, the fox calls back: "My dear raven, you have a voice — but you have no sense! Beware of those who flatter you; they want something from you."


VOCABULARY (key words)

- raven: a large black bird, similar to a crow.
- fox: a small wild dog with a bushy tail; in folk tales, the fox is often the cunning trickster.
- cheese: a food made from milk.
- beak: the hard pointed mouth of a bird.
- branch: an arm-like part of a tree.
- flatter: to praise someone in an exaggerated way, usually because you want something from them.
- pride: a feeling of being pleased with oneself; excessive self-importance.
- vain: too proud of one's appearance or qualities.
- cunning: clever in a sneaky way.
- sense: good judgement; common sense.

These words appear in many fables — in fact, the fox is the cunning trickster across most fable traditions.


COMPREHENSION POINTS

1. WHO are the characters?
   A raven (with cheese) and a fox (hungry, cunning).

2. WHAT does the fox WANT?
   The cheese the raven is holding in his beak.

3. WHY can't the fox just take the cheese?
   The raven is high up on a branch; the fox cannot climb.

4. HOW does the fox get the cheese?
   He flatters the raven, asking him to sing. When the raven opens his beak to caw, the cheese falls.

5. WHAT is the moral?
   Do not trust those who flatter you — they often want something from you.


LANGUAGE FOCUS

Pronouns. Words that replace nouns: HE (the fox), HIS (the fox's), HE (the raven), HIM (the raven). Pronouns help us avoid repeating nouns.

Direct speech. When a character speaks, we put their words in QUOTATION MARKS ("…") and start with a capital letter. Example: The fox said, "What a beautiful raven you are!"

Adjectives in description. The poem uses many: beautiful, shining, black, hungry, sly, fair, soft, sweet. Each adjective tells us how to picture the scene.

Adverbs. Words that describe how an action is done. Examples: 'a soft little PLOP' (soft = adjective; quietly would be an adverb). 'The fox snatched it up QUICKLY' — quickly is an adverb. Adverbs often end in -LY.

Rhyming words. Read the poem aloud — notice the pairs that rhyme: cheese / ease; fox / rocks; raven / heaven-(ly); air / fair; refuse / news; PLOP / hop; grin / within; praise / ways. Rhyme makes a poem easy to remember.


THINK AND WRITE

After reading, try these:

- Have you ever been flattered into doing something you wouldn't have done? Write 2-3 sentences.
- Imagine the raven later that day. What does he say to himself? Write his thoughts.
- Make a list of three OTHER stories where the fox is a trickster.
- Write your own short fable with a fox who tries to trick another animal — and give it a moral.


SUMMARY

- Aesop's fables are short stories with sharp morals; the characters are usually animals.
- "The Raven and the Fox" is about a hungry fox who flatters a vain raven into dropping a piece of cheese.
- The raven's pride makes him open his beak to caw — and the cheese falls.
- The moral: BEWARE OF FLATTERERS — those who praise you often want something from you.
- Vocabulary: raven, fox, beak, flatter, vain, pride, cunning, sense.
- Pronouns replace nouns; quotation marks mark direct speech.
- Adjectives describe nouns; adverbs (often -ly) describe actions.
- Poems rely on RHYME — pairs of words that sound similar at the end.
- The lesson applies in real life: when someone praises you a lot, ask 'what do they want?'
