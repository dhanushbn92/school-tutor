CHAPTER 2 — BIODIVERSITY REGISTER

INTRODUCTION

Walk through any park, schoolyard or village pond and you will find — if you look carefully — many living things sharing the same place. Sparrows, ants, mango trees, lichens on the bark, frogs near the water, even tiny soil creatures you cannot see. The variety of all these living things together is called BIODIVERSITY. In this chapter you will learn what biodiversity is and how to make a 'biodiversity register' — a notebook in which you record and describe the living things you find around you. Doing so trains your eyes, sharpens your scientific thinking, and helps protect what is around us.

A biodiversity register is more than a list. It captures where each living thing lives, what it eats, when it is seen and how it behaves. Local people have kept such registers for centuries — under names like 'janajeevani' or village biodiversity records — and modern India recognises them through People's Biodiversity Registers maintained by Biodiversity Management Committees.

2.1 WHAT IS BIODIVERSITY?

Bio means 'life'; diversity means 'variety'. So BIODIVERSITY is the VARIETY OF LIFE — all the living things that share an area or the planet. It is usually thought of at three levels:

• SPECIES diversity — different KINDS of plants, animals, fungi, microbes. A mango tree and a neem tree are different species; so are a sparrow and a crow.
• GENETIC diversity — different VARIETIES within one species. Different varieties of rice (basmati, sona masoori) or different breeds of cattle.
• ECOSYSTEM diversity — different KINDS OF PLACES. A forest, a river, a desert and a coral reef are different ecosystems.

India is one of the most biodiverse countries on the planet — its long coastlines, mountains, deserts, plains, rivers and forests host an enormous variety of life. Of the world's ~1.7 million known species, India is home to nearly 1 lakh (~100,000).

2.2 WHAT IS A BIODIVERSITY REGISTER?

A BIODIVERSITY REGISTER is a careful record of the living things in a chosen area, made by people who live there or visit it regularly. A school biodiversity register might cover the school campus and a 100-metre radius around it. A village register covers the whole village.

What goes into a register?
• Place — the exact location: 'beside the basketball court', 'on the school wall', 'in the back garden'.
• Habitat — what the place is like: 'shaded under trees', 'beside the drain', 'on the dry compound wall'.
• Living thing — name (local + scientific if known): 'sparrow / Passer domesticus', 'mango tree / Mangifera indica'.
• Description — colour, size, behaviour: 'small grey-brown bird with white cheeks; comes in flocks at dawn'.
• Date and time of observation.
• Use or relationship — does it help humans? eat other living things? Is it a pollinator?
• Notes / changes — what is different from last week / month?

A good register has many entries over many days. It builds slowly. It is honest — only what was actually seen, not guessed.

2.3 WHERE TO LOOK — HABITATS

DIFFERENT KINDS OF PLACES support different kinds of living things. A short list of habitats around a typical school:
• On trees and bushes — birds, squirrels, insects, lichens, tree-living plants like ferns.
• On the ground — ants, beetles, spiders, frogs after rain, fallen leaves which themselves house tiny life.
• In soil — earthworms, soil bacteria and fungi (the root partners of most plants).
• On walls and stones — spiders, gecko lizards, tiny mosses, lichens.
• In water — pondweed, water-striders, tadpoles, small fish, algae.
• In the air — flying insects, butterflies, bees, small birds.

Visit each habitat at different times of day: early morning (best for birds), noon (insects active), late afternoon (some mammals come out), and even after rain (snails, earthworms, frogs come out).

2.4 HOW TO OBSERVE AND RECORD

Good observation needs patience and the right tools.

Tools for the field:
• A small notebook and a pencil (pencil works in rain).
• A magnifying glass for tiny insects and lichens.
• A measuring tape for sizes.
• A camera or phone (with permission).
• A simple field-guide booklet of common plants and birds.

Steps for one observation:
1. Look quietly for at least 2–3 minutes; let the living thing forget you are there.
2. Note WHERE you saw it (place + habitat).
3. Note WHEN — date, time, weather.
4. SKETCH it — even a rough sketch trains the eye.
5. Note its COLOUR, SIZE and BEHAVIOUR.
6. Note SCIENTIFIC name if you can identify it; otherwise use the local name and a clear description.
7. NEVER catch, hurt or take living things. Observe; do not collect.

Be HONEST. If you are not sure what it was, say so — 'small brown bird, possibly a bulbul'. A guess marked as a guess is good science. A guess presented as a fact is bad science.

2.5 SOURCES OF INFORMATION

Your own observations are the heart of the register. But to add depth, use other careful sources:

• Local people — farmers, elders, gardeners, the school cook. They know the local names and uses of plants and animals, often better than any book.
• Field guides — illustrated booklets of birds, butterflies, trees of your region. Many are available in Hindi and other regional languages.
• School library books — encyclopaedias, atlases, NCERT science textbooks.
• Reliable websites — Wikipedia (cross-check facts), the iNaturalist app for identification, BirdLife and India Biodiversity Portal pages.
• People's Biodiversity Register of your panchayat — many local bodies maintain one.

Always RECORD where you got each piece of information. Put 'observed by me, 14 August 2026, 7 a.m.' or 'told to me by Ramaiah-ji, gardener'. This makes the register trustworthy.

2.6 TURNING OBSERVATIONS INTO A PRESENTATION

After 2–4 weeks of observation, you will have many entries. The next step: turn the register into a small PRESENTATION for your class or assembly.

Useful formats:
• A POSTER — show a sketch of the school plot, mark each habitat (tree, wall, pond), pin photos and short notes for the most interesting living things.
• A SLIDE DECK — one slide per habitat, with photos and short captions.
• A SHORT TALK — pick three favourite living things and tell a one-minute story for each: name, place, what you noticed, why you find it interesting.
• A HANDOUT — a single sheet with a list of all species seen and a small picture for each.

A good presentation also raises questions: 'Why don't we see frogs in summer?' 'Why do butterflies prefer marigolds?' Questions are the seed of more biology lessons.

2.7 WHY IT MATTERS

Why bother? Because the more we know our biodiversity, the better we can protect it. Many living things are disappearing fast — across India, frog and butterfly populations have shrunk because habitats are cleared for buildings or polluted. A school register is a small but real act of conservation: by paying attention, you make the case to keep the school plot wild and varied. Many schools have used their biodiversity register to argue against cutting an old tree, or to convince the principal to keep a corner 'unmanaged' so that wild plants and small animals can live there.

Biodiversity also matters for daily life. Bees pollinate the brinjal and tomato in our kitchen gardens. Birds eat insects that would otherwise eat crops. Earthworms make soil fertile. Lose them, and we lose all the small services they quietly provide.

SUMMARY

Biodiversity is the variety of life — at the species, genetic and ecosystem levels. A biodiversity register is a careful record of the living things in a chosen area — what they are, where they live, what they eat, and how they behave. To build one, look in different habitats (trees, ground, soil, walls, water, air) and at different times of day. Observe quietly, sketch, record place / time / weather, and never catch or hurt living things. Use local people, field guides and reliable websites for help, and always record your sources. Turn the register into a poster, slide deck or short talk to share with your school. The exercise sharpens your scientific eye — and the register is itself a small, real act of conservation.
