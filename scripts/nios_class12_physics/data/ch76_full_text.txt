CHAPTER 4 — MOTION IN A PLANE

Introduction

In Chapter 2 we described motion along a straight line — a one-dimensional problem in which "direction" was nothing more than a plus or minus sign. The real world is rarely so cooperative. A cricket ball lofted by a batter, a stone whirled at the end of a string, an aeroplane drifted off course by a crosswind — these are all motions in a plane, and to describe them honestly we need a richer language. That language is the language of vectors.

This chapter develops that language and then puts it to work on three classic two-dimensional problems: projectile motion, uniform circular motion, and motion in a moving frame (relative velocity). The tools you learn here recur in every later chapter — momentum, electric fields, magnetic forces, even the orbits of planets — so the time spent now pays back many times over.

4.1 SCALARS AND VECTORS

A scalar is a quantity that is fully specified by a single real number plus its unit. Mass (3 kg), temperature (28 °C), distance (160 m), speed (12 m/s), energy (50 J) — all scalars. A vector is a quantity that needs both a magnitude AND a direction to be fully specified. Displacement, velocity, acceleration, force and momentum are vectors. The fact that "12 m/s east" and "12 m/s north" are the same speed but different velocities is exactly what the word "vector" is there to capture.

Vectors are drawn as arrows. The length of the arrow represents the magnitude (on whatever scale is convenient); the orientation of the arrow represents the direction. We write vectors in bold (A) or with an arrow on top, and their magnitudes as A or |A|. Two vectors are equal only when both their magnitudes AND their directions agree — a vector has no "location" on the page; sliding it parallel to itself leaves it unchanged.

A unit vector is a vector of magnitude exactly one, used purely to indicate direction. The standard unit vectors along the Cartesian axes are written î, ĵ, k̂ along the x, y and z axes respectively. Any vector A in 2D can then be written A = A_x î + A_y ĵ where A_x and A_y are the (signed) x- and y-components of A.

4.2 ADDITION AND RESOLUTION OF VECTORS

To add two vectors A and B we use the triangle law: place the tail of B at the head of A; the resultant R = A + B runs from the tail of A to the head of B. The parallelogram law is the same statement drawn differently: complete the parallelogram on A and B as adjacent sides, and R is the diagonal drawn from their common tail. The two laws give the same vector.

The magnitude of R follows from the cosine rule. If A and B include an angle θ between them, then
    R² = A² + B² + 2 A B cos θ.
The direction of R relative to A is given by
    tan α = (B sin θ) / (A + B cos θ),
where α is measured from the direction of A. Two important corollaries: if A and B are parallel (θ = 0) then R is simply A + B in the same direction; if they are anti-parallel (θ = 180°) then R has magnitude |A − B| in the direction of the larger.

Resolution is the reverse process. Any vector A making angle θ with the x-axis has components A_x = A cos θ and A_y = A sin θ. Resolving turns a single 2D vector problem into two independent 1D problems — once for the x-direction, once for the y — which is precisely what makes vectors so useful. Vector subtraction A − B is just A + (−B), where −B has the same magnitude as B but the opposite direction.

The dot (scalar) product is defined as A · B = A B cos θ, a scalar. It is zero when A ⊥ B. The cross (vector) product A × B has magnitude A B sin θ and a direction perpendicular to both A and B given by the right-hand rule; it is zero when A is parallel to B.

4.3 MOTION IN A PLANE — VELOCITY AND ACCELERATION

If the position of a particle is r(t) = x(t) î + y(t) ĵ, the velocity is
    v = dr/dt = (dx/dt) î + (dy/dt) ĵ = v_x î + v_y ĵ,
and the acceleration is
    a = dv/dt = a_x î + a_y ĵ.
The 1D kinematic equations v = u + a t, s = u t + (1/2) a t², v² = u² + 2 a s carry over to two dimensions component-wise — but ONLY when a is constant in magnitude AND direction. Each Cartesian component then obeys the 1D kinematic equations independently.

This is the single most useful idea in 2D kinematics: split the motion into two 1D problems, one for each axis. Whatever happens horizontally is governed by the horizontal equations; whatever happens vertically is governed by the vertical equations; the two are linked only by the common time variable t.

4.4 PROJECTILE MOTION

A projectile is any body thrown into the air and then left to move under gravity alone (air resistance neglected). Take the launch point as the origin, the x-axis horizontal in the direction of motion, the y-axis vertical upward. If the projectile is launched with speed u at an angle θ above the horizontal, then the initial velocity components are
    u_x = u cos θ,    u_y = u sin θ.
The acceleration throughout the flight is a_x = 0 (no horizontal force) and a_y = −g (gravity downward).

Therefore at any time t:
    x(t) = u cos θ · t,
    y(t) = u sin θ · t − (1/2) g t².
Eliminating t between these gives the path of the projectile,
    y = x tan θ − (g x²) / (2 u² cos² θ),
which is a parabola opening downward — the classic projectile trajectory. Three quantities are particularly useful:

Time of flight (back to launch height):
    T = (2 u sin θ) / g.

Maximum height:
    H = (u² sin² θ) / (2 g).

Horizontal range (on a level field, returning to launch height):
    R = (u² sin 2θ) / g.

The range is maximised at θ = 45° (for which sin 2θ = 1), giving R_max = u² / g. Two complementary launch angles θ and (90° − θ) give the same range on a level surface — for example 30° and 60° both produce R = (u² sin 60°) / g, but with different times of flight and different maximum heights.

A horizontally-launched projectile (launched horizontally with speed u from a height h) is the special case θ = 0. The horizontal velocity stays u; the vertical motion is free fall from rest, so it hits the ground at time t = √(2 h / g) having travelled a horizontal distance R = u √(2 h / g).

4.5 UNIFORM CIRCULAR MOTION

When a particle moves in a circle of radius r at constant speed v, we say it is in uniform circular motion. "Uniform" refers to the speed, not the velocity — the velocity is constantly changing direction even though its magnitude is constant. A direction-changing velocity is an accelerating velocity, so there is an acceleration even in uniform circular motion.

That acceleration points toward the centre of the circle (it is therefore called centripetal acceleration, from the Latin for "centre-seeking") and has magnitude
    a_c = v² / r = ω² r,
where ω = v / r is the angular speed (radians per second). The period — the time for one complete revolution — is
    T = 2π r / v = 2π / ω.

By Newton's second law there must be a real force producing this centripetal acceleration. It is called the centripetal force and has magnitude F = m v² / r directed toward the centre. The centripetal force is NOT a new kind of force — it is the net inward force, whatever its physical origin. For a stone whirled on a string the centripetal force is the tension; for a car turning on a level road it is friction between tyres and ground; for a satellite in orbit it is gravity.

A common misconception: there is no outward "centrifugal force" acting on the particle in the laboratory frame. The outward feeling experienced by a passenger in a turning car arises because the passenger's body, by inertia, tends to continue in a straight line while the car turns inward — there is no real outward push.

4.6 RELATIVE VELOCITY IN A PLANE

If A and B are two objects with velocities v_A and v_B as seen from the ground, the velocity of A as seen from B is
    v_AB = v_A − v_B,
a vector subtraction. The magnitude and direction of v_AB depend on the angle between v_A and v_B.

Two practical problems make this concrete. River-crossing: a boat with speed v_b (relative to still water) heads across a river that flows with speed v_r relative to the bank. To cross by the shortest path (straight across), the boat must point partly upstream so that the upstream component v_b sin θ cancels the river's drag v_r; this requires sin θ = v_r / v_b and is only possible when v_b > v_r. To cross in the shortest TIME, the boat heads straight across; the river then carries it downstream, but the across-stream component is the full v_b.

Aeroplane in a crosswind: the velocity of the plane relative to the ground equals the plane's velocity relative to the air plus the air's velocity relative to the ground. A pilot wanting to fly due north when there is an east wind must crab — head the plane slightly into the wind — so that the eastward component of the airspeed cancels the wind.

Across both examples the same idea operates: velocity addition is vector addition; choose a frame, decompose into components, recompose.

SUMMARY

- A scalar needs only a magnitude; a vector needs magnitude AND direction.
- A 2D vector A = A_x î + A_y ĵ has components A_x = A cos θ, A_y = A sin θ.
- Two vectors add by the triangle / parallelogram law: R² = A² + B² + 2 A B cos θ.
- In 2D motion with constant acceleration, the x- and y- components obey the 1D kinematic equations independently.
- A projectile launched at speed u and angle θ has T = 2 u sin θ / g, H = u² sin² θ / 2 g, R = u² sin 2θ / g; range is maximised at θ = 45°.
- The trajectory of a projectile is a parabola; complementary angles give the same range.
- In uniform circular motion, the centripetal acceleration is a_c = v² / r = ω² r, directed toward the centre; the required centripetal force is F = m v² / r.
- Relative velocity in 2D is a vector subtraction: v_AB = v_A − v_B.
- River-crossing: shortest path needs sin θ = v_r / v_b upstream; shortest time means heading straight across.
