CHAPTER 3 — LAWS OF MOTION

Introduction

Kinematics describes how an object moves; dynamics asks why it moves the way it does. The bridge between them is force. Newton's three laws of motion, formulated in 1687, are the cornerstone of classical mechanics and explain everything from the trajectory of a stone thrown across a field to the orbits of the planets. In this chapter we develop the laws, apply them to typical problems involving friction and tension, and introduce momentum — a quantity whose conservation is one of the most important principles in physics.


3.1 FORCE AND INERTIA

Force. A force is a push or a pull that tends to change the state of motion of a body. Force has both magnitude and direction, so it is a vector quantity. The SI unit of force is the newton (N): 1 N is the force that produces an acceleration of 1 m/s² on a mass of 1 kg.

Inertia. Inertia is the property of a body by virtue of which it resists any change in its state of rest or of uniform motion in a straight line. The mass of the body is a quantitative measure of its inertia: a more massive body has more inertia and is harder to start, stop or turn.

Three kinds of inertia.
- Inertia of rest: a body at rest tends to remain at rest unless forced to move (the bus jolts; passengers fall backward).
- Inertia of motion: a body moving with uniform velocity tends to keep moving in a straight line unless forced to change (the bus brakes suddenly; passengers fall forward).
- Inertia of direction: a body moving in a straight line resists turning (you feel pushed outward when a car takes a sharp turn).


3.2 NEWTON'S FIRST LAW

Statement. Every body continues in its state of rest or of uniform motion in a straight line unless compelled to change that state by an external force acting on it.

Inertial frames. The first law holds only in inertial frames of reference — frames that are at rest or moving with uniform velocity (no acceleration) relative to the 'fixed stars'. A bus that brakes is a non-inertial frame; in such a frame Newton's laws appear to fail unless we introduce 'pseudo' (fictitious) forces.

Significance. The first law tells us what a force is: a force is precisely whatever changes the state of motion of a body. Without forces, motion is unchanging. The law also redefines 'rest' as a special case of uniform motion — there is no absolute rest, only motion relative to a chosen frame.


3.3 NEWTON'S SECOND LAW

Statement. The rate of change of momentum of a body is directly proportional to the net force acting on it, and takes place in the direction of the net force.

Mathematical form. If \vec{p} = m \vec{v} is the linear momentum of a body of mass m and velocity \vec{v}, then
    \vec{F}_net = d\vec{p} / dt.
For a body of constant mass this reduces to the familiar
    \vec{F}_net = m \vec{a},
where \vec{a} is the acceleration. The SI unit of force, the newton, is defined precisely so that the constant of proportionality in this equation is 1.

Important consequences.
- A net force is needed only to CHANGE the motion (start, stop, deflect or speed-change). Uniform motion in a straight line requires zero net force.
- Doubling the force on the same mass doubles the acceleration; doubling the mass under the same force halves the acceleration.
- Free-body diagram (FBD): a sketch of a single body with all the forces acting on it drawn as arrows. Solving a Newton's-second-law problem almost always begins with a careful FBD.

Linear momentum. \vec{p} = m \vec{v}. Momentum is a vector; its SI unit is kg·m/s (no special name in SI). A 1000-kg car at 10 m/s and a 100-kg car at 100 m/s have the same momentum (10 000 kg·m/s) but very different kinetic energies — a crucial distinction for collisions.

Impulse. The impulse of a force is its time integral: \vec{J} = ∫\vec{F} dt. By Newton's second law this equals the change in momentum: \vec{J} = Δ\vec{p}. So a small force applied over a long time can produce the same change in momentum as a large force applied briefly. This is why airbags, padded gloves and crumple zones reduce injury — they extend the impact time and so reduce the peak force.


3.4 NEWTON'S THIRD LAW

Statement. To every action there is an equal and opposite reaction.

Precise form. If body A exerts a force \vec{F}_(AB) on body B, then body B simultaneously exerts a force \vec{F}_(BA) = −\vec{F}_(AB) on body A. The two forces are equal in magnitude, opposite in direction, of the same kind (both gravitational, or both contact, etc.), and act on different bodies.

Important caveat. The action and reaction forces NEVER cancel each other — because they act on different bodies. For example, the Earth pulls a falling apple downward with force mg; the apple simultaneously pulls the Earth upward with the same force mg. The apple accelerates downward (its mass is small); the Earth accelerates upward (its mass is huge), so its acceleration is negligibly small — but it is not zero in principle.

Examples.
- Walking: the foot pushes the ground backward; the ground pushes the foot forward (this is what propels you).
- Recoil of a gun: the gun pushes the bullet forward; the bullet pushes the gun backward (recoil).
- Rocket propulsion: the rocket expels exhaust gas backward; the gas pushes the rocket forward.


3.5 CONSERVATION OF LINEAR MOMENTUM

Statement. In an isolated system (no external net force), the total linear momentum is conserved — it does not change with time.

Derivation. Consider two bodies that interact only with each other (no external forces). By Newton's third law, the internal forces are equal and opposite: \vec{F}_(12) = −\vec{F}_(21). The total momentum is \vec{P} = \vec{p}_1 + \vec{p}_2; its time derivative is \vec{F}_(12) + \vec{F}_(21) = 0. So \vec{P} is constant — momentum is conserved.

Applications.
- Recoil of a gun: before firing, the system (gun + bullet) is at rest, total momentum 0. After firing, the bullet moves forward with momentum \vec{p}_b; for total momentum to remain 0, the gun must move backward with momentum −\vec{p}_b. Hence v_gun = −(m_b / m_g) v_b.
- Collisions: in any collision (elastic or inelastic), if no external forces act, total momentum just before equals total momentum just after.
- Rocket: the rocket and ejected gas form an isolated system (in deep space). The rocket gains forward momentum equal in magnitude to the rearward momentum of the ejected gas.


3.6 FRICTION

Friction is the contact force that opposes the relative motion (or attempted relative motion) of two surfaces in contact.

Static and kinetic friction.
- Static friction f_s acts when the surfaces are not yet moving relative to each other; it adjusts itself to balance any applied tangential force, up to a maximum value.
- Kinetic friction f_k acts when the surfaces are sliding past one another.
- f_s ≤ μ_s N (where N is the normal force and μ_s the coefficient of static friction).
- f_k = μ_k N (kinetic friction is approximately constant, with coefficient μ_k).
- Empirically, μ_k < μ_s for the same pair of surfaces — it is harder to start sliding than to keep sliding.

Properties (approximate but adequate for this chapter).
- Friction is independent of the apparent area of contact.
- Friction is proportional to the normal force.
- Kinetic friction is approximately independent of the relative speed of sliding.

Useful and harmful. Friction lets us walk (no friction = no purchase on the ground), grip a pencil, brake a car. It also wastes energy as heat and wears out moving parts. Engineers reduce undesired friction with bearings, lubricants and rolling motion; they exploit useful friction with rough tyre treads and rubber soles.


3.7 PROBLEM-SOLVING WITH NEWTON'S LAWS

Most Newton's-laws problems follow a standard recipe:

1. Identify the body or bodies of interest (often each is treated separately, then linked by a constraint such as 'they move together' or 'the rope is inextensible').
2. Draw a free-body diagram for each body, showing every external force acting on it (gravity, normal, tension, friction, applied force).
3. Choose convenient axes — typically one along the expected motion and one perpendicular.
4. Write Newton's second law along each axis: ΣF_x = m a_x and ΣF_y = m a_y.
5. Apply constraints (e.g. the bodies have the same acceleration if connected by an inextensible rope; the normal force adjusts so the body does not penetrate the surface).
6. Solve the resulting algebraic equations for the unknowns (acceleration, tension, normal force, frictional force).

Worked example: block on a horizontal surface with friction. A block of mass m sits on a rough horizontal surface (coefficient of kinetic friction μ_k). A horizontal force F is applied. The block accelerates if F > μ_k m g; the acceleration is a = (F − μ_k m g) / m. Below this threshold the block does not move (static friction balances F).

Worked example: simple Atwood's machine. Two masses m_1 and m_2 hang from an inextensible rope over a frictionless pulley. Take m_1 > m_2 so the heavier mass falls. Acceleration a = (m_1 − m_2) g / (m_1 + m_2); tension T = 2 m_1 m_2 g / (m_1 + m_2). The same a applies to both masses (constraint).


SUMMARY

- A force is anything that changes the state of motion of a body. Inertia (= mass) measures resistance to that change.
- Newton's first law: with no net force, motion is unchanged (rest or uniform velocity).
- Newton's second law: \vec{F}_net = d\vec{p}/dt = m \vec{a} (for constant mass). Defines the newton.
- Newton's third law: action–reaction pairs are equal in magnitude, opposite in direction, and act on different bodies — they do NOT cancel.
- Linear momentum \vec{p} = m \vec{v}; impulse \vec{J} = ∫\vec{F} dt = Δ\vec{p}.
- Conservation of momentum: in an isolated system the total momentum is constant — used in collisions, recoil and rocket motion.
- Friction: static (≤ μ_s N) or kinetic (= μ_k N), with μ_k < μ_s; independent of contact area; proportional to normal force.
