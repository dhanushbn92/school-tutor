CHAPTER 1 — UNITS, DIMENSIONS AND VECTORS

Introduction

Physics is fundamentally a quantitative science. Every observation we make in the laboratory and every theoretical prediction we test rests on the measurement of physical quantities — length, time, mass, charge, force, and many more. To make a measurement meaningful we need (i) a precise definition of the quantity being measured, (ii) a fixed standard of comparison called a unit, and (iii) a clear way to express how trustworthy the result is. Beyond units, we also need a vocabulary for quantities that have a direction in space — vectors — because most laws of physics relate vector quantities like force, velocity and momentum, not just numerical magnitudes. This chapter develops the vocabulary and tools that the rest of the course assumes.


1.1 PHYSICAL QUANTITIES AND UNITS

A physical quantity is anything that can be measured. Every measurement consists of two parts: a numerical value and a unit. Writing the length of a table as 1.5 means nothing; writing it as 1.5 metres carries the full information.

Fundamental and derived quantities. Some quantities are chosen as fundamental — independent and self-defining — and all others are expressed in terms of them. In the SI system there are seven fundamental quantities: length, mass, time, electric current, thermodynamic temperature, amount of substance and luminous intensity. Quantities such as area, volume, velocity, force and energy are derived because they can be expressed as combinations of the seven fundamentals.

Units. A unit is the standard amount of a quantity used for comparison. Two quantities can be compared only when they are expressed in the same unit. Historically every region had its own units (cubit, league, seer); today the international convention is the SI system, defined and maintained by the Bureau International des Poids et Mesures.

Requirements of a good unit. A useful unit should be (i) of a convenient size for the quantity it measures, (ii) reproducible — the same anywhere on Earth and at any time, (iii) invariable, not changing with temperature, pressure or location, and (iv) well defined, with no ambiguity about what counts as one unit.


1.2 INTERNATIONAL SYSTEM OF UNITS (SI)

The SI system uses seven base units for the seven fundamental quantities, plus a set of derived units built from them.

Seven SI base units.
- Length: metre (m).
- Mass: kilogram (kg).
- Time: second (s).
- Electric current: ampere (A).
- Thermodynamic temperature: kelvin (K).
- Amount of substance: mole (mol).
- Luminous intensity: candela (cd).

Derived units. Derived units are products and quotients of the base units, often given a special name to avoid clutter:
- Area: m^2; Volume: m^3.
- Velocity: m/s; Acceleration: m/s^2.
- Force: kg·m/s^2 = newton (N).
- Pressure: N/m^2 = pascal (Pa).
- Energy / work: N·m = joule (J).
- Power: J/s = watt (W).
- Charge: A·s = coulomb (C).
- Frequency: 1/s = hertz (Hz).

SI prefixes. To handle the enormous range of magnitudes that physics deals with, SI uses standard decimal prefixes. The most common ones at the senior secondary level are:
- nano (n) = 10^(−9), micro (μ) = 10^(−6), milli (m) = 10^(−3), centi (c) = 10^(−2);
- kilo (k) = 10^3, mega (M) = 10^6, giga (G) = 10^9, tera (T) = 10^12.

So 1 km = 10^3 m, 1 nm = 10^(−9) m, 1 GHz = 10^9 Hz.


1.3 DIMENSIONS AND DIMENSIONAL ANALYSIS

The dimensions of a physical quantity describe how it depends on the seven fundamental quantities. We use square brackets and the symbols
    M = mass, L = length, T = time, A = current, K = temperature, mol = amount, cd = luminous intensity.

Examples.
- Length: [L]; area: [L^2]; volume: [L^3].
- Velocity: [L T^(−1)]; acceleration: [L T^(−2)].
- Force: [M L T^(−2)]; momentum: [M L T^(−1)].
- Work / energy: [M L^2 T^(−2)]; power: [M L^2 T^(−3)].
- Pressure: [M L^(−1) T^(−2)].
- Charge: [A T]; electric field: [M L T^(−3) A^(−1)].

Principle of homogeneity. Every term in a physically valid equation must have the same dimensions. You cannot add 1 metre to 1 second; you cannot equate kilograms with newtons. This is the most powerful sanity check in all of physics — if an equation fails the dimensional check it is wrong, full stop.

Uses of dimensional analysis.
1. Checking the correctness of an equation. For example, is v = u + at dimensionally consistent? Each term: [v] = LT^(−1); [u] = LT^(−1); [at] = (LT^(−2))·(T) = LT^(−1). All three terms have the same dimensions, so the equation is dimensionally consistent.
2. Deriving a formula up to a numerical constant. If we suspect that the period T of a simple pendulum depends only on the length L and the acceleration due to gravity g, dimensional analysis fixes T ∝ √(L/g) (the constant 2π comes from physics, not dimensions).
3. Converting from one system of units to another. Knowing the dimensions of a quantity, we know exactly how the numerical value scales when the base units change.

Limitations. Dimensional analysis cannot determine dimensionless constants (like 2π or 1/2), it cannot detect missing terms that have the same dimensions as the kept terms, and it cannot determine functional forms like exp(x) or sin(x).


1.4 SIGNIFICANT FIGURES AND ERRORS

A measurement is never exact. Every physical measurement carries an uncertainty that comes from the limitations of the instrument, the observer and the experimental procedure.

Significant figures. The significant figures (or 'sig figs') of a measurement are the digits that carry meaningful information. The number 24.50 cm has four sig figs; the trailing zero is meaningful because it indicates the result is known to that precision. The leading zeros in 0.00250 are not significant — there are three significant figures (2, 5, 0). When multiplying or dividing, the result should have the same number of sig figs as the least precise input; when adding or subtracting, the result should have the same number of decimal places as the least precise input.

Errors. The deviation of a measured value from the true value is called an error. There are three broad kinds:
1. Systematic errors — repeatable, in one direction, caused by instrument calibration or method. A miscalibrated metre stick that always reads 1 mm more than the true length introduces a systematic error.
2. Random errors — unpredictable variations from one measurement to the next, caused by uncontrolled conditions. They can be reduced (but not eliminated) by averaging many independent measurements.
3. Gross errors — outright mistakes (misreading a scale, miswriting a digit). They are reduced by training and care.

Absolute and relative error. If the mean of n measurements is x̄ and a single measurement is x_i, then |x_i − x̄| is the absolute error of that measurement. The mean absolute error Δx is the arithmetic mean of these absolute errors. The relative error is Δx / x̄, and the percentage error is 100 × Δx / x̄.

Combining errors. If a derived quantity is z = x · y or z = x / y, then the relative error in z is the sum of the relative errors in x and y. If z = x ± y, the absolute errors add (worst case). If z = x^n, the relative error in z is n times the relative error in x.


1.5 SCALARS AND VECTORS

Some quantities are completely specified by a single number with units, e.g. mass, time, temperature, energy, work — these are scalars. Other quantities require both a magnitude and a direction in space to be fully specified — these are vectors. Examples: displacement, velocity, acceleration, force, momentum, electric field. Physics problems frequently go wrong when students treat vectors as if they were scalars (or vice versa), so the first step in any problem is to identify which kind each quantity is.

Notation. A vector is written with an arrow above the symbol or in bold: \vec{a} or **a**. Its magnitude (always non-negative) is written |\vec{a}| or simply a. A unit vector is a vector of magnitude 1 in a specific direction, written â (a-hat). The standard right-handed Cartesian unit vectors are î (along x), ĵ (along y) and k̂ (along z), each of magnitude 1.

Equality of vectors. Two vectors are equal if and only if they have the same magnitude AND the same direction. Their starting points (tails) are irrelevant — vectors can be moved parallel to themselves without changing.

Types of vectors.
- Zero (null) vector: magnitude 0, direction undefined.
- Unit vector: magnitude 1.
- Equal vectors: same magnitude AND direction.
- Negative of a vector: same magnitude, opposite direction. The negative of \vec{a} is written −\vec{a}.
- Co-initial vectors: share the same starting point.
- Collinear vectors: parallel to one common line (same direction or opposite).
- Coplanar vectors: lie in one common plane.


1.6 VECTOR OPERATIONS

Addition: triangle and parallelogram laws. To add two vectors \vec{a} and \vec{b}:
- Triangle law: place \vec{b} so its tail is at the head of \vec{a}; the resultant \vec{a} + \vec{b} runs from the tail of \vec{a} to the head of \vec{b}.
- Parallelogram law: place \vec{a} and \vec{b} tail-to-tail; complete the parallelogram; the resultant is the diagonal that starts at the common tail.
Both methods give the same answer. Addition is commutative (\vec{a} + \vec{b} = \vec{b} + \vec{a}) and associative.

Subtraction. \vec{a} − \vec{b} is defined as \vec{a} + (−\vec{b}), so we negate \vec{b} first and then add.

Resolution into components. Any vector \vec{a} in the xy-plane can be written as
    \vec{a} = a_x î + a_y ĵ,
where a_x = a cos θ and a_y = a sin θ, with θ the angle measured from the +x axis. The magnitude is recovered as
    a = √(a_x^2 + a_y^2)
and the direction by tan θ = a_y / a_x. In three dimensions we add a_z k̂. Working in components turns vector arithmetic into ordinary (scalar) arithmetic on the three coordinates.

Magnitude of a sum. If two vectors \vec{a} and \vec{b} of magnitudes a and b make an angle θ between them, the magnitude of their sum is
    |\vec{a} + \vec{b}| = √(a^2 + b^2 + 2 a b cos θ).
Special cases: when θ = 0, the magnitude is a + b (max); when θ = 180°, it is |a − b| (min); when θ = 90°, it is √(a^2 + b^2).

Multiplication by a scalar. Multiplying a vector by a positive scalar k scales its magnitude by k and keeps its direction; a negative scalar reverses the direction.

Scalar (dot) product. The dot product of two vectors is the scalar
    \vec{a} · \vec{b} = a b cos θ,
where θ is the angle between them. In components: \vec{a} · \vec{b} = a_x b_x + a_y b_y + a_z b_z. The dot product is commutative and is positive when θ < 90°, zero when θ = 90° (perpendicular vectors), and negative when θ > 90°. The work done by a constant force \vec{F} on a displacement \vec{d} is W = \vec{F} · \vec{d}.

Vector (cross) product. The cross product of two vectors is the vector
    \vec{a} × \vec{b}
of magnitude a b sin θ, perpendicular to both \vec{a} and \vec{b}, with direction given by the right-hand rule (curl the fingers of the right hand from \vec{a} to \vec{b}; the thumb points along \vec{a} × \vec{b}). The cross product is anti-commutative: \vec{a} × \vec{b} = −\vec{b} × \vec{a}. It is zero when \vec{a} and \vec{b} are parallel (sin 0 = 0). The torque \vec{τ} of a force \vec{F} applied at position \vec{r} is \vec{τ} = \vec{r} × \vec{F}.


SUMMARY

- Every measurement consists of a numerical value and a unit; the SI system uses seven base units.
- Dimensions describe how a quantity depends on the seven fundamentals; every valid equation is dimensionally homogeneous.
- Significant figures and error analysis quantify how trustworthy a measurement is.
- Scalars are fully specified by magnitude alone; vectors require both magnitude and direction.
- Vectors add by the triangle/parallelogram laws and split into components for calculation.
- The dot product gives a scalar (a b cos θ); the cross product gives a vector (magnitude a b sin θ, perpendicular to both, by the right-hand rule).
