CHAPTER 2 — MOTION IN A STRAIGHT LINE

Introduction

Mechanics is the oldest branch of physics, and it begins with kinematics — the description of motion without asking what causes it. The simplest case is motion along a straight line: a car on a straight road, a stone thrown vertically upward, a ball rolling on a level track. In this chapter we develop the precise vocabulary for one-dimensional motion (position, displacement, speed, velocity, acceleration), derive the three kinematic equations that connect these quantities for uniform acceleration, and apply the framework to free fall under gravity. Throughout the chapter we use position–time and velocity–time graphs to visualise motion.


2.1 POSITION, DISTANCE AND DISPLACEMENT

Frame of reference. To describe the position of an object we first fix a frame of reference: an origin O, a direction we call positive, and a clock. The position x of the object is then the signed distance from O along that direction. If x > 0 the object is on the positive side of O; if x < 0 it is on the negative side.

Distance vs displacement. Suppose a car starts at x = 0, drives to x = 50 m, then back to x = 20 m. Its distance travelled is 50 + 30 = 80 m — distance is the total length of the path, always positive. Its displacement is x_final − x_initial = 20 − 0 = 20 m — displacement is a signed quantity (a vector in 1D, a positive or negative number) that depends only on the start and end points.

Key facts.
- Distance is a scalar; displacement is a vector.
- Distance ≥ |displacement|. Equality holds only when the motion is in a single direction throughout.
- Both quantities have SI unit metre (m).


2.2 SPEED AND VELOCITY

Average speed and average velocity. Over a time interval Δt the average speed is the distance travelled divided by Δt, and the average velocity is the displacement divided by Δt:
    average speed = (total distance) / Δt;
    average velocity = (Δx) / Δt = (x_final − x_initial) / Δt.
Average speed is a scalar; average velocity is a vector. They are NOT in general equal. For motion in a single direction the two coincide; for back-and-forth motion the average speed is larger than the magnitude of the average velocity.

Instantaneous velocity. The instantaneous velocity at time t is the limit of the average velocity over an infinitesimally small interval around t:
    v(t) = lim_(Δt → 0) Δx / Δt = dx / dt.
Geometrically, on a position–time graph, the instantaneous velocity at a point is the slope of the tangent at that point.

Instantaneous speed is the magnitude of the instantaneous velocity, |v(t)|. Instantaneous speed and instantaneous velocity always agree in magnitude.

SI unit. Both speed and velocity have SI unit metre per second (m/s).


2.3 ACCELERATION

Average acceleration. If the velocity changes from u to v over a time interval Δt, the average acceleration is
    average acceleration = (v − u) / Δt.

Instantaneous acceleration. The instantaneous acceleration at time t is the limit of the average acceleration over an infinitesimally small interval:
    a(t) = lim_(Δt → 0) Δv / Δt = dv / dt.
On a velocity–time graph, the instantaneous acceleration is the slope of the tangent.

A few cases to keep distinct.
- Uniform velocity: a = 0; the velocity is constant in magnitude AND direction.
- Uniform acceleration: a is constant in magnitude AND direction.
- Non-uniform acceleration: a itself changes with time.

Sign of acceleration. The sign of a is independent of the sign of v. An object can be slowing down with positive acceleration (e.g. a ball decelerating while moving in the negative direction has positive a but negative v).

SI unit. m/s².


2.4 KINEMATIC EQUATIONS FOR UNIFORM ACCELERATION

For motion in a straight line with constant acceleration a, initial velocity u and elapsed time t, the velocity v at time t and the displacement s during that time satisfy three equations (often called the equations of motion or 'kinematic equations'):

(1)   v = u + a t
(2)   s = u t + (1/2) a t²
(3)   v² = u² + 2 a s

These are derivable from the definitions of velocity and acceleration:
- (1) follows from a = (v − u)/t, rearranged.
- (2) follows from s = average velocity × time = ((u + v)/2) · t and substituting v from (1).
- (3) follows from eliminating t between (1) and (2): from (1), t = (v − u)/a; substitute in (2) and simplify.

These equations apply only when a is constant in time. If a varies, you must use calculus (integration) to solve the motion.

Distance vs displacement caveat. The s in equations (2) and (3) is the displacement, not the distance. For motion that reverses direction (e.g. a ball thrown up that comes back down), distance and displacement are different. Be careful which one the problem is asking about.


2.5 MOTION UNDER GRAVITY

Near the Earth's surface, every object in free fall experiences a constant downward acceleration of magnitude g ≈ 9.8 m/s² (often rounded to 10 m/s² for quick estimates). We treat gravity as a constant acceleration and apply the kinematic equations.

Convention. We typically take 'up' as the positive direction. Then the acceleration due to gravity is a = −g (always pointing down, i.e. negative). For a stone thrown upward with initial velocity u:

- At the highest point: v = 0; from (1), 0 = u − g t_up, so t_up = u/g. The stone rises for u/g seconds.
- Maximum height: from (3), 0 = u² − 2 g h_max, so h_max = u² / (2g).
- Time of flight (back to the launch height): by symmetry, t_total = 2 t_up = 2u/g.

Air resistance is ignored unless the problem explicitly says otherwise; this is the 'idealised free-fall' model.


2.6 POSITION–TIME AND VELOCITY–TIME GRAPHS

Position–time (x vs t) graph.
- A horizontal line means the object is at rest (v = 0).
- A straight line with slope m means uniform velocity v = m.
- A curve means non-uniform velocity (changing slope).
- The slope of the tangent at any point is the instantaneous velocity at that instant.

Velocity–time (v vs t) graph.
- A horizontal line means uniform velocity (a = 0).
- A straight line with non-zero slope means uniform acceleration; the slope IS the acceleration.
- The area under the curve between two times is the displacement during that interval.
- A v-t graph that crosses the time axis indicates a reversal of direction.

These graphs are powerful diagnostic tools. A typical board-exam-style question gives a v–t graph and asks you to compute displacement (area under the graph) or acceleration (slope).


SUMMARY

- Position is signed; distance is the total path length (scalar); displacement is the change in position (vector in 1D).
- Average velocity = Δx / Δt; instantaneous velocity = dx / dt = slope of x–t graph.
- Average acceleration = Δv / Δt; instantaneous acceleration = dv / dt = slope of v–t graph.
- For constant acceleration, three kinematic equations connect u, v, a, s and t: v = u + at; s = ut + (1/2)at²; v² = u² + 2as.
- Free fall near Earth: a = g ≈ 9.8 m/s² downward; h_max = u² / (2g); t_total = 2u/g (for vertical throw and return).
- The slope of an x–t graph is velocity; the slope of a v–t graph is acceleration; the area under a v–t graph is displacement.
