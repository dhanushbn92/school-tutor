CHAPTER 1 — THE RISE OF NATIONALISM IN EUROPE

INTRODUCTION

In 1848, the French painter Frédéric Sorrieu prepared a series of four prints visualising his dream of a world made up of 'democratic and social Republics'. In the first print, peoples of Europe and America — men and women of all ages and social classes — march in a long train and offer homage to the statue of Liberty as they pass by. Liberty is personified as a female figure, holding the torch of Enlightenment in one hand and the Charter of the Rights of Man in the other. Children, the dead heroes of revolutions, rise from the heavens above. From the heights look down Christ, saints and angels — symbolising fraternity among the nations.

Sorrieu's prints were not just art. They expressed a vision that was sweeping across nineteenth-century Europe — the vision of NATIONS, each made up of a free people, governing themselves. The nineteenth century in Europe was the age of NATIONALISM — the period when the modern nation-state, as we know it today, took shape.

This chapter tells that story. It begins with the French Revolution and the idea of la patrie ('the fatherland') and the citoyen ('the citizen'). It moves through Napoleon's reforms and the Vienna settlement of 1815, the wave of liberal-nationalist revolutions of the 1830s and 1848, and the unification of Italy and Germany. It closes with the Balkans, where rival nationalisms eventually plunged Europe into the First World War.

1.1 THE FRENCH REVOLUTION AND THE IDEA OF THE NATION

In 1789, the French Revolution overthrew an ABSOLUTE MONARCHY and proclaimed that sovereignty belonged not to a king, but to the PEOPLE. The Revolution declared the French as one community, with EQUAL rights and one common law. Many measures gave practical form to this idea:
• The ideas of la patrie (the fatherland) and the citoyen (the citizen) emphasised the new community of citizens enjoying equal rights under a constitution.
• A new French flag — the tricolour — replaced the royal standard.
• The Estates General was elected by the body of active citizens and renamed the National Assembly.
• A centralised administrative system put in place uniform laws for all citizens within its territory.
• Internal customs duties and dues were abolished and a uniform system of weights and measures was adopted.
• Regional dialects were discouraged, and French (as it was spoken and written in Paris) became the common language of the nation.

The revolutionaries also declared that it was the mission and the destiny of the French nation to LIBERATE THE PEOPLES OF EUROPE from despotism — to help other peoples form their nations. The arrival of French armies in Holland, Belgium, Switzerland and much of Italy in the 1790s carried this revolutionary idea of NATIONALISM with them.

1.2 NAPOLEON AND THE 'CIVIL CODE OF 1804'

In 1804, Napoleon introduced the CIVIL CODE OF 1804 — usually known as the NAPOLEONIC CODE. It did away with all privileges based on BIRTH, established EQUALITY before the law and secured the right to PROPERTY. Napoleon carried the Code to the regions under French control. In the Dutch Republic, in Switzerland, in Italy and Germany, Napoleon SIMPLIFIED ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISIONS, ABOLISHED THE FEUDAL SYSTEM, freed peasants from serfdom and manorial dues, and removed guild restrictions.

In the towns too, guild restrictions were removed. Transport and communication systems were improved. Peasants, artisans, workers and a new business class enjoyed a new-found freedom. Businessmen and small-scale producers of goods, in particular, began to realise that uniform laws, a standardised system of weights and measures, and a common national currency would FACILITATE the movement and exchange of goods and capital from one region to another.

But the new administrative arrangements went hand-in-hand with conscription, censorship, increased taxation and the loss of political freedoms. Initial enthusiasm gave way to hostility, and the French armies came to be viewed as invaders.

1.3 THE MAKING OF NATIONALISM IN EUROPE

If you looked at the map of mid-eighteenth-century Europe you would not have found 'Germany', 'Italy' or 'Switzerland'. There were KINGDOMS, DUCHIES and CANTONS, whose rulers had their own AUTONOMOUS territories. Eastern and Central Europe were under autocratic monarchies — within the Habsburg Empire of Austria-Hungary, for example, lived diverse peoples. They did not even speak the same language, but were tied together with their loyalty to the EMPEROR.

In nineteenth-century Europe, this began to change. Two new social groups led the change.

THE NEW MIDDLE CLASS. Industrialisation and the spread of education had created a new EDUCATED, LIBERAL middle class — entrepreneurs, businessmen, professionals, university teachers and students. They championed the ideas of LIBERTY (freedom for the individual and equality before the law) and a constitution that would enshrine these as fundamental rights. To them, the nation should be founded on EQUAL CITIZENSHIP — not on dynasties or empires. From this middle class came the leaders, lawyers, journalists and orators who advanced the nationalist movements.

A NEW CULTURAL OUTLOOK. Beginning in the late eighteenth century, ROMANTICISM emphasised emotions, intuition and mystical feelings. Romantic artists and poets criticised the glorification of reason and science of the Enlightenment, and instead focused on feelings, ON FOLK CULTURE. Folk songs, folk poetry and folk dances came to be seen as the true spirit of the nation (das Volk). In Germany, Johann Gottfried Herder claimed that true German culture was to be found among the COMMON PEOPLE — through their songs, poems and dances. Collecting and recording these folk traditions was an act of NATION-BUILDING. Even language carried the spirit of a nation: in Poland — partitioned among Russia, Prussia and Austria — the use of the POLISH language was treated as a weapon of national resistance.

1.4 THE AGE OF REVOLUTIONS — 1830 AND 1848

ECONOMIC HARDSHIP. The 1830s were years of great economic hardship in Europe. Population growth led to job-seekers far outnumbering employment opportunities. Population was migrating from the countryside to cities, where slums grew. Small producers in towns competed with cheap, machine-made goods from England. In regions of Europe where the aristocracy still held land, peasants struggled under the burden of feudal dues and obligations.

THE JULY REVOLUTION (FRANCE, 1830). In July 1830 Bourbon kings, who had been restored to power during the conservative reaction after 1815, were overthrown by liberal revolutionaries. They installed a constitutional monarchy with Louis Philippe at its head. Almost immediately, the news 'sparked an uprising in Brussels, which led to Belgium breaking away from the United Kingdom of the Netherlands.

GREEK STRUGGLE FOR INDEPENDENCE (1821–32). Greece, which had been part of the Ottoman Empire since the fifteenth century, struggled for independence. Nationalists in Greece got support from other Greeks living in exile and also from many West Europeans who had sympathies for ancient Greek culture. The English poet Lord Byron organised funds and went to fight in the war, where he died of fever in 1824. The Treaty of Constantinople of 1832 recognised Greece as an INDEPENDENT NATION.

THE REVOLUTIONS OF 1848. In 1848, food shortages and widespread unemployment led to a wave of uprisings across Europe — in Paris, Vienna, Berlin and Italian states. While the workers and peasants were on the streets, the educated middle classes pressed for nationalist constitutions. In FRANKFURT, an all-German National Assembly met to draft a constitution for a German nation. The proposed monarchy under Friedrich Wilhelm IV of Prussia was rejected by him; the troops were called out and the assembly was forced to disband. The 1848 revolutions were defeated almost everywhere — but they left a clear demand: a unified, constitutional, liberal nation-state.

WOMEN IN 1848. Women had formed their own political associations, founded newspapers and taken part in political meetings and demonstrations. Despite their participation, they were denied SUFFRAGE rights during the election of the Assembly. When the Frankfurt parliament convened in the Church of St Paul, women were admitted only as observers in the visitors' gallery.

1.5 THE UNIFICATION OF GERMANY AND ITALY

By the second half of the nineteenth century, NATIONALISM moved away from the LIBERAL-DEMOCRATIC sentiment of the first half and was harnessed by CONSERVATIVES for STATE-BUILDING.

GERMANY. After 1848 the task of nation-building in Germany was taken up by Prussia, under its chief minister OTTO VON BISMARCK, with the help of the Prussian army and bureaucracy. Three wars in seven years — with Denmark (1864), Austria (1866) and France (1870–71) — ended in Prussian victory. In January 1871, the Prussian king, Wilhelm I, was proclaimed German Emperor in a ceremony held at Versailles. The new state placed strong emphasis on modernising the currency, banking, legal and judicial systems in Germany.

ITALY. Italy too had a long history of political fragmentation. In the middle of the nineteenth century, Italy was divided into seven states — only one, the kingdom of Sardinia-Piedmont, was ruled by an Italian princely house. The north was under Austrian Habsburgs; the centre was governed by the Pope; the south was ruled by the Spanish Bourbons. Even the Italian language had not yet acquired one common form, with many regional and local variations.

GIUSEPPE MAZZINI sought unification through a young people's movement called 'Young Italy'. Following his failure, the responsibility was shouldered by the Sardinian king VICTOR EMMANUEL II and his minister, COUNT CAVOUR. Through diplomacy and an alliance with France, Sardinia-Piedmont defeated the Austrian forces in 1859. A large number of armed volunteers under GIUSEPPE GARIBALDI joined the fray. In 1860, they marched into South Italy and the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies and succeeded in winning the support of the local peasants in order to drive out the Spanish rulers. In 1861, Victor Emmanuel II was proclaimed king of UNITED ITALY.

1.6 THE STRANGE CASE OF BRITAIN

The model of the nation or the nation-state, some scholars have argued, EMERGED most strongly in Britain. In Britain, the formation of the nation-state was not the result of a sudden upheaval or revolution. It was the result of a long-drawn-out process. There was no British nation prior to the eighteenth century. The primary identities of the people who inhabited the British Isles were ethnic ones — such as English, Welsh, Scot or Irish. As the English nation steadily grew in wealth, importance and power, it was able to extend its influence over the OTHER nations of the islands. The English parliament, which had seized power from the monarchy in 1688, was the instrument through which a nation-state, with England at its centre, came to be forged.

The ACT OF UNION (1707) between England and Scotland resulted in the formation of the 'United Kingdom of Great Britain' — meant the English Parliament was henceforth dominated by its English members. Ireland was forcibly incorporated into the United Kingdom in 1801. A new 'British nation' was forged through the propagation of a dominant English culture. The symbols of the new Britain — the British flag (the Union Jack), the national anthem (God Save Our Noble King), the English language — were actively promoted and the older nations survived only as subordinate partners in this union.

1.7 VISUALISING THE NATION

While it was easy enough to identify a state with its rulers, attributing a face to a NATION needed visual creativity. Artists in the nineteenth century PERSONIFIED the nation; they gave the abstract idea of a nation a concrete form. Nations were portrayed as FEMALE FIGURES — known as ALLEGORIES.

FRANCE — Marianne, a popular christian name, underlined the idea of a people's nation. She was characterised by the ideas of Liberty and the Republic — the red cap, the tricolour, the cockade. Statues of Marianne were erected in public squares to remind the public of the national symbol of unity and to persuade them to identify with it. Marianne images were marked on coins and stamps.

GERMANY — Germania, who wears a crown of oak leaves, as the German oak stands for HEROISM.

The visualisation of nations as women was not just decorative. It linked the nation with the qualities thought desirable in women in nineteenth-century Europe — beauty, motherhood, courage in defence of children — and gave the nation the LOVE that a citizen owes to the patrie.

1.8 NATIONALISM AND IMPERIALISM

By the last quarter of the nineteenth century, NATIONALISM no longer retained its IDEALIST liberal-democratic sentiment of the first half of the century. It became a NARROW CREED, with limited ENDS. During this period nationalist groups became increasingly INTOLERANT of each other and ever ready to go to war. The major European powers, in turn, manipulated the nationalist aspirations of the subject peoples in Europe to FURTHER their OWN IMPERIALIST AIMS.

THE BALKANS. The Balkan region was a region of geographical and ethnic variation, comprising modern-day Romania, Bulgaria, Albania, Greece, Macedonia, Croatia, Bosnia-Herzegovina, Slovenia, Serbia and Montenegro. Inhabitants were broadly known as the SLAVS. A large part of the Balkans was under the control of the OTTOMAN EMPIRE, but the gradual breakup of the Ottoman Empire from the 1870s onwards made this region very explosive. As the different Slavic nationalities struggled to define their identity and independence, the Balkan area became an area of intense conflict.

The Balkan states were FIERCELY JEALOUS of each other and each hoped to gain more territory at the expense of the others. During this period, there was intense rivalry among the European powers — Russia, Germany, England, Austria-Hungary — over trade and colonies as well as naval and military might. These rivalries finally led to the FIRST WORLD WAR in 1914.

Anti-imperial movements that developed everywhere were nationalist movements, in the sense that they all struggled to form independent nation-states. They drew the inspiration of their movements from a Europe that had carved itself, in the previous one and a half centuries, into different nation-states. Yet the way they identified themselves and the meaning of NATION-STATE that they invented was uniquely their own.

SUMMARY

The French Revolution put forward the idea that the people, not the king, are the source of sovereignty — and that the people make a nation. Napoleon carried this nationalist idea to the rest of Europe, along with his Civil Code. After 1815, the Vienna settlement tried to put the genie back in the bottle, but it could not last. The new educated middle class and Romantic thinkers redefined the nation through ideas of liberty and folk culture. Revolutions in 1830 and 1848 demanded constitutional, liberal nation-states. Greece (1832), Belgium (1830), and finally Italy (1861) and Germany (1871), achieved unification — though through CONSERVATIVE STATECRAFT rather than democratic revolution. Britain's nation-state had a very different, slow-formed history through the Acts of Union. In the late nineteenth century, nationalism became narrower, joined hands with imperialism, and exploded in the Balkans, leading to the First World War.
