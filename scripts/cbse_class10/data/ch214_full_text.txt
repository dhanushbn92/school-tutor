CHAPTER 1 — DEVELOPMENT

INTRODUCTION

If a student of Class 10 in a small village asks, 'What does it mean for a country, or a state, or our village to be DEVELOPED?' — the answer might at first seem obvious. We point to roads, hospitals, schools, mobile phones, electric supply, more income. But ask the same question to ten different people — a farmer, a small shop-owner, a young mother, a forest-dweller, a banker — and you will get ten different answers. A farmer may say development is good rains and a fair price for crops. A young mother may say it is a good school for her child. A worker may say it is more wages and job security. The big lesson of this chapter is simple: DEVELOPMENT MEANS DIFFERENT THINGS TO DIFFERENT PEOPLE — and yet we still need fair, careful ways to compare and to plan.

The chapter teaches: (i) why people have different DEVELOPMENT GOALS; (ii) how the World Bank and the United Nations measure development; (iii) why average INCOME is a useful but limited measure; (iv) why we also need indicators on HEALTH and EDUCATION; (v) why SUSTAINABILITY of development matters.

1.1 WHAT DEVELOPMENT PROMISES — DIFFERENT PEOPLE, DIFFERENT GOALS

The textbook gives a famous example: a list of categories of people — landless rural labourer, prosperous farmer from Punjab, farmer who depends on rains, urban unemployed youth, rich urban businessman, businessman from Kerala, dam displaced person, adivasi from Narmada valley. Each category has a different idea of development.

Lessons from this list:
• Different people have DIFFERENT — sometimes CONFLICTING — development goals.
• What may be development for one may be DESTRUCTION for another. (A dam brings irrigation to a Punjab farmer, displaces an adivasi family.)
• People look at MIXES of GOALS — money, freedom, family, work, environment.
• Money is necessary but not enough. Even a rich person wants RESPECT, FREEDOM and SAFETY.

ECONOMIC DEVELOPMENT GOALS are usually about higher INCOME. Other goals are about EQUAL TREATMENT, FREEDOM, SECURITY and RESPECT — what the textbook calls NON-MATERIAL goals.

INCOME AND OTHER GOALS. More income is one of the most important goals — but quality of life depends on non-material things too. A working woman is likely to demand a safe and supportive workplace, friendly neighbours, ability to learn skills, ability to take loans on her own, regular work — not just wages.

1.2 NATIONAL DEVELOPMENT

What may be development for one MAY NOT be development for the other. Yet, when we talk about a country's development, the issue is not 'individual choices' alone. We have to think of the COMMON development of all the people. There are four big questions:
• How can a country's development be MEASURED?
• How can we COMPARE one country with another?
• What MIX OF GOALS is appropriate for the country?
• How can we ensure that development is SUSTAINABLE?

We compare different categories of people based on what is important for ALL of them — like INCOME, HEALTH and EDUCATION. We compare different countries on the same averages.

1.3 HOW TO COMPARE DIFFERENT COUNTRIES OR STATES?

For comparing countries, total INCOME is NOT a good measure. Since countries have different populations, comparing total income will not tell us what an AVERAGE person is likely to earn.

Instead, we compare AVERAGE INCOME — also called PER CAPITA INCOME. PER CAPITA INCOME is calculated as:

  per capita income = total income of the country ÷ total population

The WORLD BANK's World Development Reports use per capita income to classify countries into:
• HIGH-INCOME (RICH) countries — per capita income above US $49,300 (2022).
• LOW-INCOME (POOR) countries — per capita income below US $1,205 (2022).
• MIDDLE-INCOME countries — in between.

INDIA is in the LOW-MIDDLE income group. The threshold figures are revised over time.

1.4 INCOME — A USEFUL BUT LIMITED MEASURE

Per capita income is useful — but it has serious limitations.

First, AVERAGE INCOME HIDES INEQUALITIES. Two countries can have the same average income; in one, income is shared evenly, in another, a few are very rich and most are very poor. The textbook gives an example: Country A and Country B have the same average income (Rs 7500 per month) but very different distributions; Country B is more equal and offers better life for ordinary people.

Second, MONEY IS NEEDED FOR THINGS THAT MONEY CANNOT BUY DIRECTLY — like good public schools, clean water, public transport, a clean environment, security. These come from PUBLIC GOODS and PUBLIC SERVICES, which need collective decision and government action. So COMPARISONS based only on money miss many things.

Third, NON-MATERIAL THINGS that matter for quality of life — health, education, safety, dignity — are not captured by income alone. So we also need INDICATORS based on non-material things.

1.5 PUBLIC FACILITIES

Suppose two friends live in two states with the same average income, but State A has a state-run primary health centre, a school for every village, drinking-water supply, and electric supply; State B has none of these. Even at the same income, life in State A is better. This is because most government services are PUBLIC FACILITIES — collectively provided. Even rich people use them: in fact a rich person in State B may be poorer in real life than a less rich person in State A, because no amount of money can replace a clean public hospital and a safe public school.

Public facilities are most important for the POOR, because the poor cannot afford to buy private alternatives.

1.6 INDICATORS BEYOND INCOME

Health and education indicators give us a better picture of development. Some commonly used ones:
• INFANT MORTALITY RATE (IMR) — number of children dying per 1000 live births in a year.
• LITERACY RATE — proportion of literate population in the 7-and-above age group.
• NET ATTENDANCE RATIO — proportion of children of age 6–10 attending school as a percentage of total children of that age.
• LIFE EXPECTANCY AT BIRTH — average expected length of life of a person at the time of birth.

Compare states and countries on all these together — not just per capita income.

INDIAN STATE COMPARISON — sample (2017–18 figures):
• Per capita income — Haryana > Kerala > Bihar.
• IMR — Kerala the lowest (~7); Haryana ~30; Bihar ~32.
• Literacy rate — Kerala highest; Bihar lowest.
• Net attendance — Kerala highest.

So, Kerala has a LOWER per capita income than Haryana but BETTER human development indicators (IMR, literacy, attendance). This is because Kerala has invested more in PUBLIC HEALTH and EDUCATION.

This 'Kerala vs Haryana' lesson is the heart of the chapter: income matters, but how we USE income matters more — and human development indicators may differ from per capita income.

1.7 HUMAN DEVELOPMENT INDEX (HDI)

The UNDP (United Nations Development Programme) publishes the HUMAN DEVELOPMENT REPORT each year. It compares countries based on:
• Per capita income (in PPP US$).
• Life expectancy at birth.
• Mean and expected years of schooling (formerly literacy and gross enrolment).

These are combined into the HUMAN DEVELOPMENT INDEX (HDI) — a number between 0 and 1. Countries are ranked from highest HDI to lowest.

A SAMPLE FROM RECENT HDR (illustrative — exact figures revised each year): countries like Norway, Switzerland, Iceland top the list with HDI around 0.96. India is in the medium-human-development group with HDI around 0.63 and ranks below 130. Sri Lanka, despite a lower per capita income than some, has a HIGHER HDI than India because of better health and education outcomes.

KEY INSIGHT — UNDP measures DEVELOPMENT in terms of QUALITY OF LIFE, not income alone.

1.8 SUSTAINABILITY OF DEVELOPMENT

The story so far is about who develops and how to measure it. The remaining big question is — can we KEEP IT going for our children and grandchildren?

The Earth's resources are finite. Many resources we depend on — coal, petroleum, ground-water, forests — are NON-RENEWABLE or are used much faster than they renew. If we use them up too fast, future generations will pay the price.

GROUNDWATER. India has the world's largest groundwater extractor. In many states (Punjab, Haryana, Rajasthan, parts of Tamil Nadu), groundwater is being pumped much faster than rains can refill it. Wells go deeper and deeper, drying up. Without sustainable use, this resource will collapse.

CRUDE OIL. Reserves last only for another 35–40 years at current consumption rates. Once it ends, what will replace it for transport and plastics?

FORESTS. Reckless cutting reduces rainfall, harms biodiversity, increases erosion and warms the climate.

SUSTAINABLE DEVELOPMENT means DEVELOPMENT that meets the needs of the present WITHOUT COMPROMISING the ability of future generations to meet their own needs (Brundtland definition). It is a key concern in modern economic thinking — and a duty toward future generations.

ENVIRONMENTAL DEGRADATION crosses national borders — climate change, ozone hole, ocean pollution. Countries have to cooperate, not compete, on these issues.

SUMMARY

Development means different things to different people; income matters but so do equality, security, freedom, environment. To compare countries we use AVERAGE (per capita) INCOME — though it hides inequalities and ignores public goods. The World Bank classifies countries by income; the UNDP's Human Development Index combines income, life expectancy and education to give a fuller picture. Indian states tell their own story — Kerala has lower per capita income than Haryana, but better human development (lower IMR, higher literacy, higher net school attendance) thanks to better public health and education. Finally, development must be SUSTAINABLE — groundwater, crude oil and forests must be used carefully so that future generations have a chance.
