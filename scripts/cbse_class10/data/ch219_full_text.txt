CHAPTER 1 — A LETTER TO GOD (with the poems Dust of Snow and Fire and Ice)

INTRODUCTION

Chapter 1 of First Flight pairs a short story with two short poems — three pieces that together explore TWO of literature's biggest themes: human nature in the face of hardship, and the surprising emotional power of small things. The prose is 'A Letter to God' by the Mexican writer Gregorio López y Fuentes (1895–1966), and the poems are 'Dust of Snow' and 'Fire and Ice' by the American poet Robert Frost (1874–1963).

This chapter introduction first walks through each piece — its plot or argument, its themes, characters, language and form — then builds the connective tissue: what does the chapter as a whole teach us about human beings, faith and feeling?

1.1 'A LETTER TO GOD' — PLOT IN OUR OWN WORDS

The story is set on a small hillside farm in Mexico. The farmer LENCHO lives with his wife and children in a lonely farmhouse, the only one for miles around. From the crest of his low hill he looks down at the field of grown corn dotted with the flowers that promise a good harvest. The land needs only one thing — A GOOD SHOWER OF RAIN to bring his crop to fullness.

The rain comes that afternoon — a heavy downpour that the family welcomes. But suddenly the rain turns to HAILSTONES the size of acorns. The hailstorm continues for an hour, leaving the field WHITE as if covered with salt. Not a leaf remains on the trees; the corn is totally destroyed. Lencho's family, especially his wife and the children, gather in dismay. They have lost everything.

Lencho is no ordinary farmer. He is a man with one HOPE that does not depend on the weather: he believes that GOD WILL HELP. That night he sits down and writes a LETTER TO GOD asking for ONE HUNDRED PESOS so that he can plant his fields again and live until the new harvest. He addresses the envelope simply 'To God' and posts it.

The next day a postman finds the letter and takes it to the POSTMASTER, laughing as he shows it to him. The postmaster — a fat, jovial man — laughs at first, but soon his expression changes. He takes the letter seriously: he does not want to SHAKE the writer's faith in God. He decides that something must be done. He asks his employees to contribute, and personally adds part of his own salary. He gathers SEVENTY PESOS — less than the hundred Lencho asked for, but the most they can manage. He puts the money in an envelope addressed to Lencho and signs it simply 'GOD'.

The next Sunday Lencho arrives at the post office, asks if there is a letter for him, takes the envelope, opens it — and counts. Seventy pesos. He shows no gratitude, only ANGER. Sitting down, he writes another letter to God, complaining that he has received only seventy pesos when he asked for a hundred. He suspects, he writes, that the rest was STOLEN BY THE POST OFFICE EMPLOYEES — 'a bunch of crooks'. He posts this second letter and leaves.

The story ends on this twist of IRONY — the very people who, out of compassion, contributed their own money to help Lencho are accused by him of being thieves. The reader sees what Lencho cannot.

1.2 THEMES OF 'A LETTER TO GOD'

(a) UNSHAKEABLE FAITH. Lencho's faith is the engine of the story. He does not pray, give up or beg from neighbours; he writes directly to God. His faith is so simple and so absolute that even the postmaster does not want to disturb it.

(b) HUMAN COMPASSION. The postmaster and his employees are not religious figures. They are ordinary people in a small post office. Yet they respond to Lencho's faith with practical compassion: they collect money for him, including from their own pockets.

(c) IRONY. The story's central technique is irony. The 'God' who answers Lencho's prayer is in fact a group of compassionate human beings. Lencho mistakes their help for an act of God — and then mistakes them for crooks. The reader sees through both errors. Irony is the gap between what the character thinks and what the reader knows.

(d) PERCEPTION VS REALITY. Lencho's certainty in his own world-view is so total that he cannot even consider an alternative explanation for the missing thirty pesos. Many of us are like Lencho: trapped inside one lens through which the world makes sense to us.

(e) NATURE — FRIEND AND FOE. The story opens with the hill, the field, the breeze and the smell of rain — nature as the farmer's partner. Then a single hailstorm destroys his crop. Nature is BOTH life-giver AND destroyer.

1.3 CHARACTERS IN 'A LETTER TO GOD'

LENCHO — a hard-working farmer of strong faith. He is hopeful, persistent and decisive. Yet he is also stubborn and SUSPICIOUS — incapable of imagining that ordinary humans could have helped him. The story names him: 'Lencho' is a familiar form of 'Lorenzo' in Mexican Spanish, suggesting an everyman.

LENCHO'S WIFE — appears briefly. She is the practical voice in the family, calling the children in from the rain and lamenting the destruction.

THE POSTMASTER — a 'fat, amiable' fellow. He laughs at the letter at first but then becomes serious; his decisions drive the second half of the story. He represents practical human kindness.

THE POSTMEN AND POSTAL EMPLOYEES — anonymous helpers. They contribute money from their own pockets in response to Lencho's faith. Without them there would be no story of a 'reply'.

1.4 LANGUAGE AND CRAFT

López y Fuentes's prose is simple, almost folkloric. The narrator describes the farm with the cadence of a fable; the storm arrives without warning; the post office scenes are SLIGHTLY HUMOROUS — the postmaster's laugh, the men gathering money, the addressing of the envelope as 'GOD'. The story uses simple repetition (the letter, the reply, the second letter) and a single sharp ironic twist. The whole story is short — the kind of fable that can be read aloud in twenty minutes — yet it lingers.

Vocabulary to know: HAILSTORM (storm of small ice balls); CRESTFALLEN (sad and disappointed); RESOLUTE (determined, firm); DELUGE (heavy rain or flood); CONVICTION (firm belief); CORRESPONDENCE (letters exchanged); INK-WELL (small pot of ink for a fountain pen); AMIABLE (good-tempered, friendly).

1.5 'DUST OF SNOW' — PARAPHRASE OF MEANING

Robert Frost's 'Dust of Snow' (published 1923) is a very short poem of just eight lines. It tells of an ordinary moment in a New-England winter that quietly changes the speaker's mood. While walking under a HEMLOCK TREE — a tree with strong, sometimes gloomy associations in literature — the speaker is suddenly dusted by a small fall of snow, knocked off a branch by a CROW. That tiny, accidental gift — cold flakes on his face from a bird often associated with bad luck on a tree often associated with sadness — somehow LIFTS HIS HEART. A part of a day he had thought 'wasted' is given back to him.

The poem is a single sentence in two stanzas: a 'simple gift' offered by an unlikely source. It does not explain. It does not moralise. It just records the moment and the change.

1.6 THEMES OF 'DUST OF SNOW'

(a) THE POWER OF SMALL THINGS. A grand event is not needed to lift a sad mood. A snowflake falling from a bird's wing can be enough. The poem is a small example of a large idea — that joy can come from the most unexpected, unimportant places.

(b) NATURE AS HEALER. The crow and the hemlock — both often seen as gloomy in literary tradition — TURN OUT to be the speaker's healers. Nature does not always provide grand sunsets; sometimes a brief moment of cold air and ice-dust is enough.

(c) UNEXPECTED REDEMPTION. The poem is structured as a contrast. Before: the speaker has 'rued' (regretted) the day. After: he feels his mood lift. The change comes UNBIDDEN — without effort or seeking on his part.

(d) SIMPLICITY. The poem itself is a small thing — eight short lines, simple words, a single sentence — and that simplicity is part of its message: small things, simply observed, can carry great meaning.

1.7 LANGUAGE AND CRAFT OF 'DUST OF SNOW'

The poem has TWO STANZAS of FOUR LINES each. Frost uses a tight RHYME scheme — ABAB CDCD — and short, clipped lines. The two key images are the CROW (typically a symbol of bad luck or death in folklore) and the HEMLOCK TREE (a poisonous evergreen — in classical antiquity, hemlock was the poison given to Socrates). By choosing these two 'dark' images and yet using them to DELIVER joy, Frost performs a quiet REVERSAL — he tells us that even the gloomy parts of life and nature can deliver unexpected blessings.

Note: the poem does NOT mean Frost was suicidal or that the speaker was about to give up. It's a small moment of MOOD CHANGE — relevant to anyone who has had a bad morning lifted by a small thing.

Vocabulary: HEMLOCK (a poisonous evergreen tree); RUED (regretted); BORE (carried).

1.8 'FIRE AND ICE' — PARAPHRASE OF ARGUMENT

Frost's 'Fire and Ice' (published 1920) is also short — just nine lines — but it asks a vast question: HOW WILL THE WORLD END? The poem is a kind of argument. There are two camps: those who say the world will end in FIRE, and those who say it will end in ICE. The speaker first sides with the FIRE camp, drawing on what he has FELT of DESIRE — the hot, consuming kind. But, he says, if the world had to end TWICE, he knows enough of HATE to say that ICE would do for destruction too — ice being slow, cold, deadening.

So the poem links two NATURAL forces — fire and ice — to two HUMAN emotions — DESIRE and HATE. Both, the speaker says, are destructive enough to end a world. One is hot and quick, the other cold and slow; the result is the same.

1.9 THEMES OF 'FIRE AND ICE'

(a) HUMAN EMOTIONS AS DESTRUCTIVE FORCES. Frost's bigger point: the FORCES that can destroy the world are not natural disasters at all. They are HUMAN — the desires that consume, the hates that freeze. We make our own apocalypse.

(b) FIRE = DESIRE. Burning, consuming, all-or-nothing wants. Greed; lust; ambition without limit.

(c) ICE = HATE. Cold, slow, deliberate. Indifference, bitterness, vengeance, the hardening of the heart.

(d) BOTH ARE EQUAL THREATS. Frost does not say one is worse than the other. He weighs them carefully and finds that BOTH would work. The reader is invited to consider which one operates in their own life.

(e) THE BREVITY OF JUDGEMENT. In just nine short lines Frost delivers a meditation on cosmic and personal destruction — the kind of statement that other poets might take a hundred lines to make.

1.10 LANGUAGE AND CRAFT OF 'FIRE AND ICE'

The poem has nine short lines. It uses casual, conversational language — 'Some say', 'From what I've tasted of desire', 'I think I know enough of hate'. Frost rhymes freely (ABAABCBCB or similar) but never lets the rhyme dominate the meaning. The CONTRAST between the two destructive forces is the structural backbone. The voice is calm — almost philosophical — making the apocalyptic content even more powerful.

Vocabulary: PERISH (be destroyed; die); SUFFICE (be enough).

1.11 PUTTING THE PIECES TOGETHER

Why does the textbook pair these three pieces?

A LETTER TO GOD is about FAITH and IRONY — a man's deep religious certainty meets the practical compassion of strangers. The story sees CHARACTERS in motion through a small drama.

DUST OF SNOW is about UNEXPECTED LIFT — a tiny moment of nature changing a person's mood for the better.

FIRE AND ICE is about UNEXPECTED DESTRUCTION — how human emotions, almost invisibly, can destroy a world.

Together they explore HUMAN NATURE in small encounters — with weather, with strangers, with one's own moods, with one's own emotions. Across all three, the message is the same: SMALL THINGS — a hailstorm, a snowflake, a feeling — have a power FAR LARGER than they appear. And humans, with all our faith, our compassion and our destructive emotions, are the centre of it all.

1.12 STUDY GUIDE — KEY POINTS

- 'A Letter to God' was originally written in Spanish by Gregorio López y Fuentes; it is a famous example of MAGICAL REALISM in Latin American literature, where the everyday meets the miraculous.
- The chapter's central technique in the prose is IRONY — what the character believes vs what the reader knows.
- The poems are by Robert Frost, an American poet famous for using rural New England as a setting for universal themes.
- 'Dust of Snow' uses the SYMBOLS of the crow and the hemlock — usually associated with bad luck — to deliver an unexpected JOY.
- 'Fire and Ice' uses two PHYSICAL FORCES to STAND IN FOR two destructive HUMAN EMOTIONS.
- All three pieces are SHORT — meant to be read in minutes — yet each carries a large theme.

SUMMARY

In the prose 'A Letter to God', a faithful Mexican farmer Lencho writes to God for 100 pesos after a hailstorm destroys his crop; the postmaster and his staff, moved by his faith, collect 70 pesos as a reply 'from God'; Lencho, on receiving the money, writes a second letter complaining that the post office has stolen the missing thirty pesos. The story is a study in faith, compassion and ironic perception. In 'Dust of Snow', Frost shows how a tiny natural moment (a crow shaking snow on the speaker) can transform a mood. In 'Fire and Ice', he argues that the world's two great destructive forces — DESIRE (fire) and HATE (ice) — are not natural disasters but HUMAN emotions. Together the chapter explores the surprising reach of small events and inner forces in human life.
