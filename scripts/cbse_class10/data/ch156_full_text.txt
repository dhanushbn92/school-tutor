CHAPTER 2 — ACIDS, BASES AND SALTS

Introduction

Curd is sour. Soap is slippery and bitter. Lemon juice and tamarind taste sour; baking soda is bitter. These familiar tastes tell us that the kitchen is full of ACIDS and BASES. In chemistry the difference between an acid and a base is precise — and understanding that difference unlocks why the dabba of pickle stays fresh, why soap cleans your hands, and why our stomach can digest food without burning itself. This chapter explains the chemistry of acids, bases and salts, and how the pH scale measures their strength.


2.1 ACIDS AND BASES — INTRODUCTION

ACIDS are substances that taste SOUR. Examples in your kitchen: lemon juice (citric acid), tamarind (tartaric acid), vinegar (acetic acid), curd (lactic acid), tomatoes (oxalic acid). Stronger acids in the lab: hydrochloric acid (HCl), sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄), nitric acid (HNO₃).

BASES are substances that taste BITTER and feel SOAPY (slippery). Examples: soap, washing soda, ammonia, magnesium hydroxide (in milk-of-magnesia tablets). Strong lab bases: sodium hydroxide (NaOH), potassium hydroxide (KOH), calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)₂).

WARNING: never taste an unknown chemical. Use INDICATORS instead. An indicator is a substance that changes colour in acid or base.

Common indicators.
- Litmus paper: red in acid, blue in base.
- Methyl orange: red in acid, yellow in base.
- Phenolphthalein: colourless in acid, pink in base.
- Universal indicator: gives a colour from RED (strong acid) through GREEN (neutral) to PURPLE (strong base) — basis of the pH scale.

Olfactory indicators (smell-based) include onion juice and clove oil — they smell different in acid vs base. Useful for visually-impaired students.


2.2 CHEMICAL PROPERTIES OF ACIDS

Acids share three characteristic chemical reactions.

A. Acids react with METALS to give a SALT and HYDROGEN GAS.
    Acid + Metal → Salt + H₂↑.
- Zn + 2 HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂↑ (a colourless gas; burns with a 'pop' sound).
- 2 Na + 2 HCl → 2 NaCl + H₂↑ (very vigorous).
Not all metals react — gold and silver, for instance, are too unreactive.

B. Acids react with METAL CARBONATES and METAL HYDROGENCARBONATES to give a salt, water, and CO₂.
    Acid + Metal carbonate → Salt + H₂O + CO₂↑.
- 2 HCl + CaCO₃ → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂↑ (limestone fizzing in vinegar).
- HCl + NaHCO₃ → NaCl + H₂O + CO₂↑.
The CO₂ gas turns lime water (Ca(OH)₂) milky — this is the lab test for CO₂.

C. Acids react with METALLIC OXIDES (which are basic) to give a salt and water — neutralisation.
    Acid + Metal oxide → Salt + H₂O.
- 2 HCl + CuO → CuCl₂ + H₂O (the blue-green CuCl₂ gives the colour change).


2.3 CHEMICAL PROPERTIES OF BASES

Bases also share characteristic reactions, mirroring acids.

A. Bases react with NON-METALLIC OXIDES (which are acidic) to give a salt and water.
    Base + Non-metallic oxide → Salt + H₂O.
- 2 NaOH + CO₂ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O (CO₂ is acidic; soaks up in NaOH).

B. Bases react with ACIDS — neutralisation. Acid + Base → Salt + Water.
- HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O.
- H₂SO₄ + 2 KOH → K₂SO₄ + 2 H₂O.

C. Some bases react with metals (e.g., zinc with NaOH) to give a salt and hydrogen.
- Zn + 2 NaOH → Na₂ZnO₂ + H₂↑.
This is less common — only certain metals react with bases this way.


2.4 WHAT DO ACIDS AND BASES HAVE IN COMMON?

When dissolved in water, ACIDS produce HYDROGEN IONS (H⁺), and BASES produce HYDROXIDE IONS (OH⁻).
- HCl(aq) → H⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq).
- NaOH(aq) → Na⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq).

ALL acids share the H⁺ ion → so they share similar properties.
ALL (alkaline) bases share the OH⁻ ion → so they share similar properties.

H⁺ ions cannot exist alone in water; they immediately bond with water molecules to form HYDRONIUM ions (H₃O⁺). So strictly:
    HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻.

Acid solutions conduct electricity because of these ions; pure HCl gas, however, does not conduct because there are no free ions.

Diluting an acid is HIGHLY exothermic. Always add ACID TO WATER, never water to acid — adding water to concentrated acid can cause the acid to splash out due to the heat released.

Concentrated vs dilute. CONCENTRATED means a large amount of acid (or base) per litre of solution. DILUTE means a small amount — usually because you have added water to the concentrated solution.

Strong vs weak. A STRONG acid (or base) IONISES COMPLETELY in water — every molecule splits into ions. A WEAK acid (or base) IONISES PARTIALLY — only a small fraction splits.
- Strong acids: HCl, H₂SO₄, HNO₃.
- Weak acids: CH₃COOH (vinegar), citric acid, carbonic acid.
- Strong bases: NaOH, KOH.
- Weak base: NH₃ (ammonia).

Note: 'concentrated' and 'strong' are NOT the same thing. You can have a dilute strong acid or a concentrated weak acid.


2.5 THE pH SCALE

The pH scale measures how acidic or basic a solution is. It runs from 0 to 14.
- pH 0 to 7: acidic (lower number = more acidic).
- pH 7: NEUTRAL (pure water).
- pH 7 to 14: basic / alkaline (higher number = more basic).

Each unit of pH represents a TEN-FOLD change in H⁺ ion concentration. So pH 3 has 10 times more H⁺ than pH 4, and 100 times more than pH 5.

pH of common substances.
- Lemon juice ≈ 2; gastric (stomach) juice ≈ 1-2.
- Vinegar ≈ 3; tomato juice ≈ 4.
- Coffee ≈ 5; rain water ≈ 6.
- Pure water = 7.
- Blood ≈ 7.4; sea water ≈ 8.
- Baking soda solution ≈ 9; soap ≈ 10.
- Household ammonia ≈ 11; bleach ≈ 12.
- Drain cleaner ≈ 14.

Importance of pH in everyday life.
- Living organisms function only within a narrow pH range. Human blood pH must stay near 7.4.
- Plants grow best in soil with a specific pH range; very acidic or basic soil hurts crops. Farmers add lime (calcium oxide) to neutralise acidic soil.
- Tooth decay: when pH in the mouth falls below 5.5 (after sugary food), the enamel starts to dissolve. Toothpastes are basic — they neutralise excess acid.
- ACID RAIN (pH below 5.5 due to dissolved SO₂, NO₂) damages buildings, plants and water bodies.
- Self-defence: when stung by a bee, the sting injects acid; rubbing baking soda (a mild base) neutralises and soothes. When stung by a wasp, the sting is alkaline; vinegar (an acid) helps.


2.6 SALTS

A SALT is the product of the reaction between an acid and a base. Different acid + base combinations give different salts.

Family of salts. Salts that share the same parent acid OR the same parent base form a 'family'. Examples of the chloride family: NaCl, CaCl₂, KCl. Examples of the sodium family: NaCl, Na₂SO₄, Na₂CO₃.

pH of salt solutions.
- Salt of strong acid + strong base (e.g. NaCl from HCl + NaOH): NEUTRAL (pH ≈ 7).
- Salt of strong acid + weak base (e.g. NH₄Cl): ACIDIC (pH < 7).
- Salt of weak acid + strong base (e.g. CH₃COONa): BASIC (pH > 7).

Common salt — sodium chloride (NaCl).
- Sea water is a major source.
- Used in cooking and food preservation.
- Raw material for many industrial chemicals.

From common salt — three important industrial chemicals.

A. Sodium hydroxide (NaOH) — the chlor-alkali process.
    2 NaCl + 2 H₂O → 2 NaOH + Cl₂↑ + H₂↑ (electrolysis of brine).
- NaOH is used in soap making.
- Cl₂ is used in PVC, disinfectants.
- H₂ is used as fuel and in fertilisers.

B. Bleaching powder, Ca(OCl)₂.
    Ca(OH)₂ + Cl₂ → CaOCl₂ + H₂O.
- Used to bleach cotton, paper.
- Used to disinfect drinking water.

C. Baking soda (sodium hydrogencarbonate, NaHCO₃).
    NaCl + H₂O + NH₃ + CO₂ → NaHCO₃ + NH₄Cl.
- Used in baking — heat releases CO₂, making cakes rise.
- Mild antacid (relieves acidity).
- An ingredient in fire extinguishers.

D. Washing soda (sodium carbonate decahydrate, Na₂CO₃·10 H₂O).
    Na₂CO₃ + 10 H₂O → Na₂CO₃·10 H₂O.
- Used in washing detergents (softens hard water).
- Used in glass and paper industries.

E. Plaster of Paris, CaSO₄·½ H₂O. Made by heating gypsum (CaSO₄·2 H₂O) at 373 K (100 °C):
    CaSO₄·2 H₂O → CaSO₄·½ H₂O + 1½ H₂O.
- When mixed with water, hardens to gypsum again — used for casts, moulds, decorative work.

F. Hydrated salts and water of crystallisation. Some salts have water molecules in their crystal structure — e.g., copper sulphate pentahydrate (CuSO₄·5 H₂O) is blue. On heating, it loses the water and turns white. Adding water restores the blue colour.


SUMMARY

- Acids taste sour; bases taste bitter and feel soapy. Indicators (litmus, methyl orange, phenolphthalein) detect them.
- Acids react with metals → salt + H₂; with carbonates → salt + H₂O + CO₂; with metallic oxides → salt + H₂O.
- Bases react with non-metallic oxides → salt + H₂O; with acids → salt + H₂O (neutralisation); some with metals → salt + H₂.
- Acids in water release H⁺ ions; bases release OH⁻ ions. Neutralisation: H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O.
- Always add ACID TO WATER (never water to acid) when diluting.
- Strong acids ionise completely; weak acids ionise partially.
- pH scale 0-14: < 7 acidic, = 7 neutral, > 7 basic. Each unit = 10× change in H⁺.
- pH governs soil, blood, tooth decay, acid rain.
- Salts come from acid + base reactions; common salt → NaOH, bleaching powder, baking soda, washing soda, plaster of Paris.
- Hydrated salts have water of crystallisation; CuSO₄·5 H₂O is blue, CuSO₄ alone is white.
