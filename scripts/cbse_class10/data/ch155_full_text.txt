CHAPTER 1 — CHEMICAL REACTIONS AND EQUATIONS

Introduction

Look around you. Milk turns into curd. An iron nail rusts. A piece of paper burns. The food you eat is digested. In every one of these examples, ONE substance is changing into ANOTHER substance — that is, a CHEMICAL REACTION is taking place. Chemistry studies these reactions: how to recognise them, how to write them down compactly, how to balance them so that no atom is created or destroyed, and how to classify them into a few useful types.


1.1 CHEMICAL EQUATIONS

A WORD EQUATION describes a reaction in words. For example, magnesium burns in air to form magnesium oxide:
    Magnesium + Oxygen → Magnesium oxide.

A CHEMICAL EQUATION uses chemical formulas instead of words. The same reaction is written:
    Mg + O₂ → MgO.

The substances on the LEFT (Mg and O₂) are the REACTANTS. The substance(s) on the RIGHT (MgO) is/are the PRODUCT(S). The arrow → reads "gives" or "yields".

Observations that suggest a chemical reaction has happened:
- A change in colour (e.g. blue copper sulphate reacting with iron turns to greenish-blue iron sulphate).
- Evolution of a gas (bubbles fizzing out).
- Formation of a precipitate (a solid that settles out).
- Change in temperature (the test tube becomes hot or cold).
- Change in state (a solid becomes liquid, etc.).

If at least one of these is observed, a chemical reaction is likely.


1.2 BALANCED CHEMICAL EQUATIONS

The LAW OF CONSERVATION OF MASS states that mass cannot be created or destroyed in a chemical reaction. In a chemical equation, this means: the number of atoms of each element on the LEFT must equal the number on the RIGHT. An equation that satisfies this is a BALANCED equation.

Look at Mg + O₂ → MgO. On the left we have 1 Mg, 2 O. On the right we have 1 Mg, 1 O. The oxygens don't match — this equation is NOT balanced.

To balance it: place a 2 in front of MgO to give 2 oxygens on the right; then add 2 in front of Mg on the left to give 2 magnesiums:
    2 Mg + O₂ → 2 MgO.
Now: 2 Mg = 2 Mg ✓, 2 O = 2 O ✓. Balanced.

Procedure for balancing.
1. Write the unbalanced equation.
2. Count atoms of each element on both sides.
3. Use COEFFICIENTS (numbers in front of formulas) to balance — never change subscripts.
4. Start with the element appearing in only one substance on each side; balance hydrogen and oxygen last.
5. Recount to verify.

Symbols sometimes added.
- (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, (aq) aqueous (dissolved in water).
- ↑ for gas evolved, ↓ for precipitate.
- Δ above the arrow for "with heating".
- A catalyst's name above the arrow if relevant.

Example. Combustion of methane:
    CH₄ + 2 O₂ → CO₂ + 2 H₂O.


1.3 TYPES OF CHEMICAL REACTIONS

Most reactions fall into one of five types.

A. COMBINATION REACTION. Two or more reactants combine into ONE product.
    A + B → AB.
- 2 Mg + O₂ → 2 MgO (magnesium burning).
- C + O₂ → CO₂.
- CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ (slaking lime; very exothermic).

B. DECOMPOSITION REACTION. ONE reactant breaks down into two or more products.
    AB → A + B.
- 2 H₂O₂ → 2 H₂O + O₂ (hydrogen peroxide decomposing — gives oxygen gas).
- CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ (limestone heated — quicklime + carbon dioxide).
- 2 Pb(NO₃)₂ → 2 PbO + 4 NO₂ + O₂.

Decomposition driven by HEAT is THERMAL decomposition. By LIGHT — PHOTOCHEMICAL (e.g. silver chloride turns grey in sunlight: 2 AgCl → 2 Ag + Cl₂). By ELECTRICITY — ELECTROLYTIC (e.g. electrolysis of water).

C. DISPLACEMENT REACTION. A more reactive element displaces a less reactive element from its compound.
    A + BC → AC + B.
- Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu (iron displaces copper).
- Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu.
The reactivity order helps predict whether a displacement happens.

D. DOUBLE DISPLACEMENT REACTION. Two compounds exchange ions.
    AB + CD → AD + CB.
- AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl↓ + NaNO₃ (white precipitate of silver chloride).
- Na₂SO₄ + BaCl₂ → BaSO₄↓ + 2 NaCl.

E. OXIDATION-REDUCTION (REDOX). Oxidation = gain of oxygen OR loss of hydrogen. Reduction = loss of oxygen OR gain of hydrogen. Both happen together — one substance is oxidised while another is reduced.
- 2 Cu + O₂ → 2 CuO. Copper is OXIDISED (gains oxygen).
- CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O. Here CuO is REDUCED (loses oxygen) and H₂ is oxidised (gains oxygen). So this single reaction is both an oxidation and a reduction — a REDOX reaction.


1.4 EFFECTS OF OXIDATION REACTIONS IN EVERYDAY LIFE

A. CORROSION. The slow eating-away of metals by air, moisture and chemicals.
- Iron rusts: Fe + O₂ + H₂O → rust (Fe₂O₃·xH₂O), reddish-brown.
- Copper turns green when exposed to moist air.
- Silver tarnishes black (silver sulphide).
Prevention: painting, oiling, galvanising (coating with zinc), tin plating, alloying (e.g. stainless steel = Fe + Cr + Ni).

B. RANCIDITY. Oils and fats in food slowly oxidise in air, causing bad smell and taste — the food turns RANCID.
Prevention: airtight packaging, refrigeration, addition of antioxidants (BHA, BHT), flushing packets with inert gases like nitrogen (e.g. potato-chip packets).

The same idea — oxidation — drives both: the slow rusting of an iron gate, and the slow spoiling of a packet of biscuits left open. Reducing exposure to oxygen / moisture is the common cure.


SUMMARY

- A chemical reaction converts reactants into products; observations include colour, gas, precipitate, temperature change.
- Chemical equations use formulas; balanced equations have equal atoms of each element on both sides (law of conservation of mass).
- Types of reactions: combination, decomposition, displacement, double displacement, redox (oxidation-reduction).
- Decomposition can be thermal, photochemical or electrolytic.
- Displacement obeys the reactivity series — more reactive metal displaces less reactive one.
- Double displacement often produces a precipitate.
- Oxidation = gain of O / loss of H; reduction = the reverse; they always happen together.
- Corrosion (rust, tarnish, green coating on copper) and rancidity (food spoilage) are everyday consequences of oxidation.
