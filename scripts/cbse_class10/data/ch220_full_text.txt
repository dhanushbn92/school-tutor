CHAPTER 2 — NELSON MANDELA: LONG WALK TO FREEDOM (with the poem A Tiger in the Zoo)

INTRODUCTION

Chapter 2 of First Flight pairs an excerpt from one of the great political autobiographies of the twentieth century with a short poem about a caged tiger. The prose is from 'Long Walk to Freedom' by NELSON MANDELA (1918–2013), written in prison and published in 1994 — Mandela's account of his life and of the long South African struggle against apartheid. The poem is 'A Tiger in the Zoo' by the contemporary British poet LESLIE NORRIS (1921–2006).

Both pieces explore FREEDOM — what it is, why it matters, how it can be won and what its loss feels like. Mandela's prose tells the story from the OUTSIDE — the first day of South Africa's freedom, the inauguration ceremony, and the long road that brought him there. Norris's poem looks at freedom from the OPPOSITE side — through the eyes of a tiger trapped in a cage.

This chapter introduction first walks through Mandela's piece, then the poem, and finally the connections between them.

2.1 'LONG WALK TO FREEDOM' — THE INAUGURATION DAY

The textbook excerpt opens on 10 MAY 1994 at the Union Buildings in Pretoria. It is the day Nelson Mandela is INAUGURATED as the first BLACK PRESIDENT of South Africa. Mandela describes the day with a calm, careful eye: the sandstone amphitheatre, the dignitaries from all over the world, the moment when he and his daughter Zenani are joined on the stage by his deputies — F.W. de Klerk and Thabo Mbeki — and Mandela takes the oath of office.

For South Africa, this is more than a swearing-in. Three centuries of WHITE RULE — colonial conquest, segregation, and finally the brutal system of APARTHEID — are being formally brought to an end. The new flag is raised. The world's leaders are present. Mandela is called by his clan name 'Madiba'. The South African Air Force performs a fly-past in the new colours.

The most striking moment for Mandela is the AFRICAN national anthem — 'Nkosi Sikelel' iAfrika' — sung to the world. Until very recently, the singing of this anthem in public would have been a CRIME for him. Now it is the anthem of his free, democratic, multi-racial nation.

2.2 WHAT MANDELA SAID — KEY IDEAS

Mandela's inauguration speech and the surrounding pages explore several big ideas. The textbook excerpt highlights these:

(a) THE END OF AN ERA. Mandela calls 10 May 1994 'this day, so deeply felt by the millions of our people'. It is the day that ends 'an extraordinary human disaster' — apartheid. He pledges that 'never, never and never again shall it be that this beautiful land will again experience the oppression of one by another'.

(b) HONOURING THE STRUGGLE. Mandela does not present his presidency as a personal achievement. He honours the THOUSANDS of his comrades — known and unknown — who fought, were imprisoned and died for freedom. He says that the policies of apartheid created 'a deep and lasting wound' on his country and his people, but that the same struggle gave South Africa 'men of such extraordinary courage, wisdom and generosity'.

(c) DUAL OBLIGATIONS. Mandela talks about 'twin obligations' that every man has in life: an OBLIGATION TO HIS FAMILY (his parents, wife and children) and an OBLIGATION TO HIS PEOPLE (his community and his country). In a free society, each person can fulfil both. In apartheid South Africa, a black man could fulfil neither — to do his duty to his people forced him into conflict with the state, often at the cost of his family.

(d) THE NATURE OF COURAGE. Mandela says he learned from his comrades that 'courage was not the absence of fear, but the triumph over it'. The brave man is not he who DOES NOT feel afraid; the brave man is he who CONQUERS that fear.

(e) ON LOVE AND HATE. Mandela says no one is born hating another person because of his skin or his background. People LEARN to hate — and if they can learn to hate, they can be taught to LOVE, for love comes more naturally to the human heart than its opposite.

(f) WHAT FREEDOM REALLY MEANS. As a young boy in his Transkei village, Mandela thought of freedom as the right to run, swim, plough, ride and walk the village — the FREEDOM OF A BOY. As a young man in Johannesburg he learned that freedom is more — the right to live a life of dignity, marry, raise a family, follow a profession. As an adult, he came to understand freedom as a struggle for an ENTIRE PEOPLE — that no man is free until ALL are free, including those who deny others their freedom. THE OPPRESSOR IS AS UNFREE AS THE OPPRESSED, since both are imprisoned by hatred and prejudice.

(g) THE LONG WALK. Mandela ends the excerpt with the title image: a long walk, with hills behind him and many more hills ahead. He has walked some way; he now sees the long climb still to come. There can be no rest, he says — to be FREE is not merely to cast off one's chains, but to live in a way that respects and ENHANCES the freedom of others.

2.3 SOUTH AFRICA AND APARTHEID — A SHORT BACKGROUND

To understand the chapter, the reader needs a brief background of the country.

SOUTH AFRICA is a country at the southern tip of Africa. Its land is rich in gold and diamonds, which made it a colony first of the Dutch (from 1652) and then of the British. As white settlers grew in number and political power, they built a system that progressively kept BLACK AFRICANS, COLOURED PEOPLE and INDIANS away from political power, good land, decent education and good jobs.

In 1948, after the white-supremacist National Party won a general election, this system was formalised as APARTHEID — a word that means 'separateness' in Afrikaans. Apartheid laws controlled where black people could live, work, study, walk and love. Blacks were forced to carry passes, were removed from cities to far-off 'homelands', and were denied the vote. Schools, hospitals, parks and beaches were strictly segregated by race.

Resistance to apartheid was organised through the AFRICAN NATIONAL CONGRESS (ANC) and other groups. Mandela joined the ANC as a young lawyer; he led nonviolent and later armed resistance, was tried for sabotage, and on 12 June 1964 was sentenced to LIFE IMPRISONMENT. He was held mostly on Robben Island for 27 years, doing hard labour in a quarry and writing his autobiography in secret.

International pressure (sanctions, sports boycotts) and continued internal resistance forced the white-supremacist government to negotiate. In 1990 Mandela was RELEASED. In 1991 apartheid laws were repealed. The first multi-racial democratic election was held on 27 April 1994; the ANC won with a clear majority; on 10 May 1994 Mandela was sworn in as the first black President. He served one term (1994–99) and died in 2013.

2.4 THEMES OF 'LONG WALK TO FREEDOM'

(a) FREEDOM. The whole chapter is about freedom — from the boy's freedom of the village to the adult's freedom of the citizen, to the larger freedom of an entire people, to the deep insight that no one is truly free if any are unfree.

(b) STRUGGLE AND SACRIFICE. Mandela honours the men and women — his comrades, family, even ordinary villagers — who paid the price for freedom. The presidency is a tribute to them.

(c) FORGIVENESS WITHOUT FORGETTING. Mandela does not denounce the white community — he embraces former enemies in his cabinet (de Klerk, his deputy president). But he also does not paper over the wound of apartheid. He calls it 'an extraordinary human disaster'.

(d) UNITY. Mandela's speech and gestures — black and white together on the stage, the African anthem and the old anthem sung side by side, fly-pasts and military salutes — emphasise a NEW NATIONAL IDENTITY that includes all South Africans.

(e) COURAGE. Mandela's idea that 'courage is the triumph over fear, not the absence of it' is one of the most quoted lines from the autobiography.

(f) UBUNTU. Mandela does not use the word in this excerpt, but the African concept of 'ubuntu' — 'I am because we are' — runs through it. A person is fully human only with and through other people.

2.5 LANGUAGE AND CRAFT OF MANDELA'S PROSE

Mandela writes a CALM, REPORTED prose. The narration is mostly in past tense — he is reflecting back on a great day. The vocabulary is simple but the sentences are LONG and CAREFULLY BALANCED, often using parallel structure ('to fight, to be imprisoned, to die'). He uses CONCRETE imagery (sandstone amphitheatre, the new flag, the fly-past) to ground big ideas.

He also uses several MEMORABLE PHRASES that travel beyond the chapter:
• 'the time for healing of wounds has come'
• 'never, never and never again'
• 'the policy of apartheid created a deep and lasting wound'
• 'I learned that courage was not the absence of fear, but the triumph over it'
• 'no one is truly free if all are not free'

Vocabulary to know: APARTHEID (system of strict racial separation in South Africa); INAUGURATION (formal ceremony to start a term in office); EMANCIPATION (the act of being set free); UNINTENDED CONSEQUENCES; RESILIENCE (ability to bounce back from hardship); UBUNTU (African concept of common humanity).

2.6 'A TIGER IN THE ZOO' — PARAPHRASE OF MEANING

Leslie Norris's poem 'A Tiger in the Zoo' (published 1969) is FIVE STANZAS of four lines each — twenty lines total. It looks at freedom from the OPPOSITE side: an unfree creature.

The poem moves between two scenes. STANZAS 1, 3 and 5 show the tiger IN HIS CAGE — pacing, watching, growling, staring at the moon. STANZAS 2 AND 4 show a CONTRASTING image: the tiger as he WOULD BE in the wild — sliding through long grass, lurking near a water-hole to ambush deer, or terrorising a village by snarling near its huts.

The contrast is the poem's central craft. The CAGED tiger pads SLOWLY back and forth on PADS OF VELVET, behind BARS OF HIS CAGE. He IGNORES visitors. At night, when the cars are gone and the city is silent, he hears the patrolling car AND the silence and stares at the BRILLIANT STARS, longing for a freedom he cannot have.

The poet does not tell us we should free the tiger. He just shows us, in carefully alternated stanzas, what is LOST when a wild creature is caged.

2.7 THEMES OF 'A TIGER IN THE ZOO'

(a) FREEDOM AND CAPTIVITY. The poem is a meditation on what FREEDOM means for a wild creature — and, by extension, for any caged being.

(b) NATURE VS HUMAN-MADE WORLD. The wild scenes (long grass, water-hole, forest, village) are the tiger's RIGHT environment. The cage is a HUMAN-MADE world that diminishes him.

(c) DIGNITY IN CAPTIVITY. Despite the cage, the tiger has a kind of dignity — he ignores visitors, paces with care, watches the stars at night. Captivity has reduced him but not erased him.

(d) CRUELTY OF ZOOS. The poem is an indirect criticism of the zoo as an institution. Norris does not lecture; he just SHOWS.

(e) THE POEM AS COUNTERPART TO MANDELA. The 27-year prisoner Mandela had a daily knowledge of cages. The tiger's longing for the wild is a small mirror of Mandela's longing for South Africa.

2.8 LANGUAGE AND CRAFT OF 'A TIGER IN THE ZOO'

Norris uses simple, vivid language. Several techniques are notable:

(i) ALTERNATING STANZAS — caged scene / wild scene / caged scene / wild scene / caged-at-night. This structural rhythm makes the contrast inescapable.

(ii) STRONG VERBS — pads, slides, lurks, snarls, terrorises, hears, stares. The verbs do most of the work.

(iii) RHYME — Norris rhymes the second and fourth lines of each stanza (ABCB pattern), creating a quiet melody without overpowering the meaning.

(iv) IMAGES — pads of velvet (softness), bars of cage (hardness), brilliant stars (beauty out of reach). The images contrast soft with hard, freedom with confinement, distance with closeness.

(v) THE LAST WORD. The poem ends with the tiger 'staring at the brilliant stars'. The stars are FREE — distant, eternal, unreachable. The tiger's gaze is the gaze of every prisoner.

Vocabulary: STALKS (walks proudly, stealthily); PADS (soft cushions on a cat's foot; or the act of walking softly); LURKS (waits hidden); SNARLS (makes a fierce growl); TERRORISES (frightens); BAR-LIKE ('like the bars of a cage'); BRILLIANT (very bright).

2.9 PUTTING THE TWO PIECES TOGETHER

The chapter pairs Mandela and Norris because they explore the same theme — FREEDOM — from opposite poles.

MANDELA writes from the FREEDOM SIDE — the day a long imprisonment ends, the long walk that brought him to it, the long walk still ahead. The piece is FACTUAL, RESPONSIBLE, public — a great person speaking about a great event.

NORRIS writes from the CAPTIVITY SIDE — through the imagined eye of a tiger pacing in a cage, longing for jungle, watching the stars. The piece is QUIET, SHORT, intimate — a small image of a large idea.

Together they tell us: FREEDOM is not a small thing. To win it for an ENTIRE people takes the work of generations and a long walk. To LOSE it — even for a single creature — is to lose oneself, even if one paces with the dignity of a tiger.

The chapter also reminds us of one of Mandela's most powerful insights: the OPPRESSOR is as UNFREE as the OPPRESSED. The cage diminishes the JAILER as well as the prisoner. A society that imprisons its own people imprisons itself.

SUMMARY

In the prose excerpt from 'Long Walk to Freedom', Nelson Mandela describes his inauguration as the first black President of South Africa on 10 May 1994 and reflects on the long struggle against apartheid. Key ideas: the end of three centuries of white rule; the honouring of comrades who paid the price for freedom; the twin obligations of family and people; the nature of courage as the triumph over fear, not its absence; and the deeper meaning of freedom — that no one is free until all are free. Leslie Norris's poem 'A Tiger in the Zoo' looks at freedom from the opposite side — a caged tiger who paces in his cage, contrasted with the wild tiger he would be — long grass, water-hole, village. The two pieces together explore freedom as the largest theme of human life.
