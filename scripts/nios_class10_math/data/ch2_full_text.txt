CHAPTER 2 — EXPONENTS AND RADICALS

Introduction

In mathematics, we often deal with numbers that are multiplied by themselves a number of times. Writing such expressions repeatedly is cumbersome. The notation of exponents (or indices) provides a compact way of representing such repeated multiplication, while the closely related notion of radicals captures the inverse operation of taking roots. In this chapter we develop the laws of exponents for positive integers, extend them to zero, negative and rational exponents, and study radicals as n-th roots. We end with the simplification of radical expressions and the technique of rationalising denominators.


2.1 POSITIVE INTEGRAL EXPONENTS

If a is any real number and n is a positive integer, then
    a^n = a × a × a × ... × a (n factors of a).
Here a is called the base and n is called the exponent (or index, or power). The expression a^n is read as "a raised to the power n" or simply "a to the n".

Examples.
    2^3 = 2 × 2 × 2 = 8.
    (−3)^4 = (−3) × (−3) × (−3) × (−3) = 81.
    (1/2)^5 = 1/32.
    a^1 = a, by convention.

A few useful conventions and observations:
- a^1 = a for every real number a.
- The product of an even number of negative factors is positive; the product of an odd number is negative. So (−a)^n = a^n if n is even and (−a)^n = −a^n if n is odd.
- If a > 0, then a^n > 0 for every positive integer n.
- 0^n = 0 for every positive integer n; 0^0 is left undefined.

Laws of exponents (positive integers). For any real numbers a, b and positive integers m, n:

Law 1 (Product law):  a^m × a^n = a^(m+n).
Law 2 (Quotient law): a^m ÷ a^n = a^(m−n) provided a ≠ 0 and m > n.
Law 3 (Power of a power): (a^m)^n = a^(m×n).
Law 4 (Power of a product): (a × b)^n = a^n × b^n.
Law 5 (Power of a quotient): (a/b)^n = a^n / b^n provided b ≠ 0.

Each law follows directly from the definition by counting factors. For example, in Law 1, on the left-hand side we have m + n factors of a in total, which is exactly a^(m+n). These laws are central — every simplification of an exponent expression in this chapter ultimately reduces to one of them.


2.2 ZERO AND NEGATIVE INTEGRAL EXPONENTS

To extend exponents beyond positive integers we look for definitions that make the laws above continue to hold. The product law a^m × a^n = a^(m+n) forces a particular choice for a^0 and for a^(−n).

Zero exponent. Setting m = 0 and n = 1 in the product law gives
    a^0 × a^1 = a^(0+1) = a^1.
Cancelling a^1 (allowed because a ≠ 0) gives a^0 = 1.

Convention. a^0 = 1 for every non-zero real number a.

The expression 0^0 is left undefined.

Negative exponents. Setting m = −n and n = n in the product law and using a^0 = 1:
    a^(−n) × a^n = a^0 = 1, so a^(−n) = 1 / a^n.

Convention. For a ≠ 0 and n a positive integer,
    a^(−n) = 1 / a^n.

With these definitions, all five laws of exponents continue to hold for any integer exponents (positive, zero or negative).

Examples.
    5^0 = 1.
    2^(−3) = 1 / 2^3 = 1/8.
    (3/4)^(−2) = (4/3)^2 = 16/9.
    10^(−1) = 1/10 = 0.1.
    a^(−5) × a^7 = a^(−5+7) = a^2 (using the product law).


2.3 RATIONAL EXPONENTS

Once integer exponents are well defined, the next step is to define a^p where p is a rational number, say p = m/n with n a positive integer.

The n-th root. If a ≥ 0, the n-th root of a, written a^(1/n) or n√a, is the unique non-negative real number x such that x^n = a. So
    (a^(1/n))^n = a.
For a < 0, the n-th root is defined when n is odd; for n even, n√a is undefined in the real numbers (e.g. √(−4) is not a real number).

Rational exponents. With a > 0 and m, n integers, n > 0:
    a^(m/n) = (a^(1/n))^m = (a^m)^(1/n) = n√(a^m).

That is, a^(m/n) is the n-th root of a, raised to the power m. Equivalently, it is the n-th root of a^m. Both interpretations give the same value when a > 0.

The five laws of exponents continue to hold for rational exponents whenever the bases are positive:
    a^(p+q) = a^p × a^q,
    a^(p−q) = a^p / a^q,
    (a^p)^q = a^(p×q),
    (a × b)^p = a^p × b^p,
    (a/b)^p = a^p / b^p.

Examples.
    8^(1/3) = 2 (because 2^3 = 8).
    16^(3/4) = (16^(1/4))^3 = 2^3 = 8.
    9^(−1/2) = 1 / 9^(1/2) = 1/3.
    25^(3/2) = (25^(1/2))^3 = 5^3 = 125.
    (32/243)^(2/5) = (32^(1/5) / 243^(1/5))^2 = (2/3)^2 = 4/9.


2.4 RADICALS AND SURDS

A radical is an expression that uses the radical sign √. The general form is
    n√a = a^(1/n),
where a ≥ 0 (or n is odd) and n is a positive integer. The number n is called the index of the radical and a is the radicand. When n = 2 we usually drop the index and write √a; this is the square root.

A surd is a radical n√a in which a is a positive rational number whose n-th root is irrational. For example, √2 is a surd because 2 is rational but √2 is not. By contrast, √4 = 2 is rational and is therefore not a surd.

Order of a surd. The order of n√a is n. So √2 is a surd of order 2 (a quadratic surd), and 3√5 is a surd of order 3 (a cubic surd).

Like and unlike surds. Two surds are said to be like if they have the same index and the same radicand after simplification. Otherwise they are unlike. For example 2√3 and 5√3 are like; √3 and 3√3 (cube root of 3) are unlike because they have different indices.

Pure and mixed surds. A pure surd has 1 as its rational coefficient, e.g. √7. A mixed surd has a non-trivial rational coefficient, e.g. 3√2.

Laws of radicals. For positive real numbers a, b and positive integers m, n:
    n√(a × b) = n√a × n√b,
    n√(a / b) = n√a / n√b (b ≠ 0),
    m√(n√a) = (m × n)√a.

Examples.
    √50 = √(25 × 2) = √25 × √2 = 5√2.
    3√24 = 3√(8 × 3) = 3√8 × 3√3 = 2 × 3√3 = 2 · 3√3.
    √(2/9) = √2 / √9 = √2 / 3.


2.5 SIMPLIFICATION OF RADICALS

A radical n√a is in simplified form when:
1. The radicand a contains no perfect n-th-power factor other than 1. So inside √ we should have no perfect square; inside 3√ we should have no perfect cube; and so on.
2. The radicand contains no fraction.
3. There is no radical in the denominator of any fraction (this is handled by rationalisation, see §2.6).

Examples (square roots).
    √72 = √(36 × 2) = 6√2.
    √200 = √(100 × 2) = 10√2.
    √(75 / 4) = √75 / √4 = (5√3) / 2.

Examples (cube roots).
    3√54 = 3√(27 × 2) = 3 × 3√2.
    3√(40 / 27) = 3√40 / 3√27 = (2 × 3√5) / 3.

Operations on radicals. Two like surds can be added or subtracted by combining their coefficients:
    p × √a + q × √a = (p + q) × √a.

Multiplication and division of surds with the same index:
    (p × m√a) × (q × m√b) = (p × q) × m√(a × b),
    (p × m√a) / (q × m√b) = (p / q) × m√(a / b).

Surds with different indices must be brought to a common index before such operations. To compare or combine √2 and 3√3, raise each to a common index 6:
    √2 = 6√(2^3) = 6√8,    3√3 = 6√(3^2) = 6√9,
so 3√3 > √2.


2.6 RATIONALISATION OF DENOMINATORS

When a fraction has a surd in its denominator we usually rewrite it so that the denominator is rational. This process is called rationalisation, and the multiplier we use is called the rationalising factor.

Single-term denominator. A denominator of the form √a is rationalised by multiplying numerator and denominator by √a, since √a × √a = a.
Example: 5 / √2 = (5 × √2) / (√2 × √2) = (5 × √2) / 2.

Two-term denominator (binomial surds). A denominator of the form a + b√c is rationalised by multiplying by its conjugate a − b√c, since
    (a + b√c)(a − b√c) = a^2 − b^2 × c,
which is rational. Similarly, the conjugate of √a + √b is √a − √b, and
    (√a + √b)(√a − √b) = a − b.

Examples.
    1 / (3 + √5) = (3 − √5) / ((3 + √5)(3 − √5)) = (3 − √5) / (9 − 5) = (3 − √5) / 4.
    1 / (√7 − √3) = (√7 + √3) / ((√7 − √3)(√7 + √3)) = (√7 + √3) / (7 − 3) = (√7 + √3) / 4.
    (2 + √3) / (2 − √3) = (2 + √3)^2 / ((2 − √3)(2 + √3)) = (4 + 4√3 + 3) / (4 − 3) = 7 + 4√3.

Why we rationalise. A rationalised denominator is in standard form for arithmetic, makes decimal approximations easier (you only divide by a rational number), and is the convention expected by most exam solutions.


SUMMARY

- a^n with n a positive integer means a multiplied by itself n times.
- a^0 = 1, a^(−n) = 1/a^n, a^(m/n) = n√(a^m), all defined to keep the laws of exponents consistent.
- The five laws of exponents — product, quotient, power-of-a-power, power-of-a-product, power-of-a-quotient — extend from positive integers to all rational exponents (with bases > 0).
- A radical n√a equals a^(1/n). A surd is an irrational radical.
- Simplification removes perfect n-th-power factors from the radicand and any fraction inside the radical.
- Rationalisation removes radicals from the denominator using a single √a factor or, for binomial surds, the conjugate.
