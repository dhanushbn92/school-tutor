CHAPTER 6 — QUADRATIC EQUATIONS

Introduction

In Class 9 we studied linear equations of the form ax + b = 0 in one variable, where the unknown appears only to the first power. Many real-world problems — projectile motion, the area of a rectangle of given perimeter, profit-maximisation in business, the time taken to fill a tank, or the relation between speed and distance — lead instead to an equation in which the unknown appears to the second power. Such an equation is called a quadratic equation. In this chapter we make the idea precise, develop three standard methods for solving quadratic equations (factorisation, completing the square, and the quadratic formula), study the role of the discriminant in determining the nature of the roots, and finally apply quadratic equations to short word problems.


6.1 INTRODUCTION TO QUADRATIC EQUATIONS

A quadratic equation in one variable x is an equation that can be written in the standard form
    a x^2 + b x + c = 0,
where a, b, c are real numbers and a ≠ 0. The condition a ≠ 0 is essential — without it the x^2 term disappears and the equation reduces to the linear equation b x + c = 0.

The number a is the leading coefficient, b is the coefficient of x, and c is the constant term. The expression a x^2 + b x + c is called a quadratic polynomial in x.

Examples.
    2 x^2 − 5 x + 3 = 0       (a = 2, b = −5, c = 3).
    x^2 − 4 = 0                (a = 1, b = 0, c = −4).
    3 x^2 + 7 x = 0            (a = 3, b = 7, c = 0).
    (x − 2)(x + 5) = 0         (in standard form: x^2 + 3 x − 10 = 0).
    x^2 = 25                   (in standard form: x^2 − 25 = 0).

Equations that do not appear quadratic at first sight may become quadratic after simplification:
    (x − 1)(x + 1) + 4 = 0     →  x^2 + 3 = 0.
    x + 6/x = 5                →  x^2 − 5 x + 6 = 0  (multiply through by x, x ≠ 0).
    √(x + 4) = x − 2           →  x + 4 = (x − 2)^2 = x^2 − 4 x + 4  →  x^2 − 5 x = 0.

Whenever we transform an equation by multiplying by an unknown factor, by squaring, or by clearing fractions, we may introduce extraneous roots — values that satisfy the transformed equation but not the original. Every solution must therefore be checked in the original equation.

Roots (or solutions) of a quadratic equation. A root of a x^2 + b x + c = 0 is a value of x that makes the left-hand side equal to zero. A quadratic equation has at most two real roots. Geometrically, the real roots are the x-coordinates of the points where the graph of y = a x^2 + b x + c (a parabola) crosses the x-axis.


6.2 SOLUTION BY FACTORISATION

If we can factorise a x^2 + b x + c into two linear factors,
    a x^2 + b x + c = (p x + q)(r x + s),
then the equation (p x + q)(r x + s) = 0 holds exactly when at least one factor is zero — this is the zero-product property of real numbers. Setting each factor to zero gives the two roots
    x = −q / p   or   x = −s / r.

The "split-the-middle-term" method. To factorise a x^2 + b x + c we look for two numbers whose product is a × c and whose sum is b. Splitting b into those two parts and grouping reveals the linear factors.

Example 1. Solve x^2 − 7 x + 12 = 0.
    a × c = 1 × 12 = 12; we need two numbers with product 12 and sum −7. The pair (−3, −4) works.
    x^2 − 3 x − 4 x + 12 = 0
    x (x − 3) − 4 (x − 3) = 0
    (x − 3)(x − 4) = 0
    x = 3 or x = 4.

Example 2. Solve 6 x^2 + x − 15 = 0.
    a × c = 6 × (−15) = −90; need product −90 and sum 1. The pair (10, −9) works.
    6 x^2 + 10 x − 9 x − 15 = 0
    2 x (3 x + 5) − 3 (3 x + 5) = 0
    (3 x + 5)(2 x − 3) = 0
    x = −5/3 or x = 3/2.

Example 3. Solve x^2 − 9 = 0.
    (x − 3)(x + 3) = 0   (difference of squares)
    x = 3 or x = −3.

Example 4. Solve 4 x^2 = 25.
    4 x^2 − 25 = 0  →  (2 x − 5)(2 x + 5) = 0  →  x = ±5/2.

Factorisation is the fastest method when the coefficients are small integers and the splitting-pair is easy to spot. When no integer pair works, the next two methods always succeed.


6.3 SOLUTION BY COMPLETING THE SQUARE

The method of completing the square rewrites a x^2 + b x + c as a perfect square plus a constant. The key identity is
    x^2 + 2 p x = (x + p)^2 − p^2.
That is, to make x^2 + 2 p x into a perfect square we add p^2 — half of the coefficient of x, then square it.

Algorithm to solve a x^2 + b x + c = 0 (a > 0) by completing the square:
1. Divide both sides by a so the leading coefficient is 1: x^2 + (b/a) x + c/a = 0.
2. Move the constant to the right: x^2 + (b/a) x = −c/a.
3. Add (b / 2a)^2 to both sides: x^2 + (b/a) x + (b/2a)^2 = (b/2a)^2 − c/a.
4. The left-hand side is a perfect square: (x + b/(2a))^2 = (b^2 − 4 a c) / (4 a^2).
5. Take square roots: x + b/(2a) = ± √(b^2 − 4 a c) / (2a).
6. Solve for x: x = (−b ± √(b^2 − 4 a c)) / (2a).

Steps 5 and 6 require b^2 − 4 a c ≥ 0; if it is negative the equation has no real roots.

Example 1. Solve x^2 − 6 x + 5 = 0 by completing the square.
    x^2 − 6 x = −5
    x^2 − 6 x + 9 = −5 + 9
    (x − 3)^2 = 4
    x − 3 = ±2
    x = 5 or x = 1.

Example 2. Solve 2 x^2 + 4 x − 6 = 0 by completing the square.
    Divide by 2: x^2 + 2 x − 3 = 0.
    x^2 + 2 x = 3
    x^2 + 2 x + 1 = 3 + 1
    (x + 1)^2 = 4
    x + 1 = ±2
    x = 1 or x = −3.

Why this method matters. Completing the square is more than just another technique — it is the derivation that gives us the quadratic formula in the next section, and it is the standard tool for converting a parabola into vertex form.


6.4 QUADRATIC FORMULA (SRIDHARACHARYA FORMULA)

Carrying out the completing-the-square steps once and for all on the general equation a x^2 + b x + c = 0 (a ≠ 0) gives the quadratic formula:

    x = (−b ± √(b^2 − 4 a c)) / (2 a).

This formula works for every quadratic equation. It was known in essentially this form to the 9th-century Indian mathematician Sridharacharya, and the formula is sometimes called the Sridharacharya formula in his honour.

The expression D = b^2 − 4 a c is called the discriminant of the equation; it tells us how many real roots there are and is studied in the next section.

To use the quadratic formula:
1. Write the equation in standard form a x^2 + b x + c = 0 and identify a, b, c.
2. Compute D = b^2 − 4 a c.
3. If D ≥ 0, substitute into x = (−b ± √D) / (2 a) to get the two roots (which coincide if D = 0).
4. If D < 0, report that the equation has no real roots.

Example 1. Solve 2 x^2 − 7 x + 3 = 0 by the quadratic formula.
    a = 2, b = −7, c = 3.
    D = (−7)^2 − 4 × 2 × 3 = 49 − 24 = 25, so √D = 5.
    x = (7 ± 5) / 4
    x = 3   or   x = 1/2.

Example 2. Solve x^2 + 4 x + 1 = 0.
    a = 1, b = 4, c = 1; D = 16 − 4 = 12.
    x = (−4 ± √12) / 2 = (−4 ± 2 √3) / 2 = −2 ± √3.

Example 3. Solve 3 x^2 − 6 x + 4 = 0.
    a = 3, b = −6, c = 4; D = 36 − 48 = −12 < 0.
    No real roots.

Sum and product of the roots. If α and β are the roots of a x^2 + b x + c = 0, the formula gives
    α + β = −b / a,
    α × β = c / a.
These two relations are useful for forming a quadratic equation when the roots are known: for given roots p and q,
    x^2 − (p + q) x + p q = 0.


6.5 NATURE OF ROOTS — THE DISCRIMINANT

The discriminant D = b^2 − 4 a c determines the nature of the roots of a x^2 + b x + c = 0.

Three cases.
- D > 0:  the roots are real and distinct (the parabola crosses the x-axis at two points).
- D = 0:  the roots are real and equal (a repeated root x = −b / (2 a); the parabola just touches the x-axis).
- D < 0:  the roots are not real; they are complex conjugate numbers (the parabola lies entirely above or below the x-axis and does not meet it).

The roots are rational when D is a perfect square (and a, b, c are rational); otherwise the real roots are irrational and occur as conjugate surds p + √q and p − √q.

Examples.
    3 x^2 − 5 x + 2 = 0:  D = 25 − 24 = 1 > 0 (perfect square)  →  two distinct rational roots.
    x^2 − 4 x + 4 = 0:    D = 16 − 16 = 0  →  one repeated real root x = 2.
    x^2 + x + 1 = 0:      D = 1 − 4 = −3 < 0  →  no real roots.
    x^2 − 2 x − 2 = 0:    D = 4 + 8 = 12 > 0 (not a perfect square)  →  two distinct irrational roots, x = 1 ± √3.

Using the discriminant to find a missing parameter. We can also use D to find a value of an unknown coefficient that produces a desired nature of roots. For example, the equation k x^2 − 4 x + 1 = 0 has equal roots iff D = 0, i.e. 16 − 4 k = 0, giving k = 4. For this k the repeated root is x = 4 / (2 × 4) = 1/2.


6.6 WORD PROBLEMS ON QUADRATIC EQUATIONS

A standard word problem leading to a quadratic equation is solved in four steps:
1. Read carefully and identify the unknown; assign a letter (usually x) to it.
2. Translate the conditions of the problem into an equation in x. Often this equation will be quadratic.
3. Solve the quadratic equation by any of the methods of §§6.2–6.4.
4. Reject roots that do not make sense in the context (negative lengths, ages or speeds; impossible counts).

Example 1 (number-relation). The sum of a number and its reciprocal is 13 / 6. Find the number.
    Let the number be x (x ≠ 0). Then x + 1/x = 13/6.
    Multiplying by 6 x: 6 x^2 + 6 = 13 x  →  6 x^2 − 13 x + 6 = 0.
    Using the quadratic formula or factorisation: (3 x − 2)(2 x − 3) = 0  →  x = 2/3 or x = 3/2.
    Both numbers satisfy the original equation; in fact 3/2 = 1 / (2/3), so they are reciprocals of each other.

Example 2 (geometry — area). The area of a rectangle is 60 m^2 and its length is 4 m more than its breadth. Find the dimensions.
    Let the breadth be x m. The length is (x + 4) m. Then x (x + 4) = 60.
    x^2 + 4 x − 60 = 0  →  (x + 10)(x − 6) = 0  →  x = 6 (reject x = −10).
    Breadth = 6 m, length = 10 m.

Example 3 (motion — speed and time). A train travels 360 km at a uniform speed. If the speed had been 5 km/h more, it would have taken 1 hour less for the same journey. Find the original speed of the train.
    Let the original speed be x km/h (x > 0). Original time = 360 / x hours.
    New time at speed (x + 5) km/h is 360 / (x + 5) hours, which is 1 hour less.
    360 / x − 360 / (x + 5) = 1
    360 (x + 5) − 360 x = x (x + 5)
    1800 = x^2 + 5 x  →  x^2 + 5 x − 1800 = 0.
    Using the quadratic formula: D = 25 + 7200 = 7225 = 85^2.
    x = (−5 + 85) / 2 = 40 (reject the negative root).
    Original speed = 40 km/h.

Example 4 (age problem). The product of Asha's age (in years) five years ago and her age eight years from now is 30. Find her present age.
    Let Asha's present age be x years (x ≥ 5).
    (x − 5)(x + 8) = 30
    x^2 + 3 x − 40 = 30  →  x^2 + 3 x − 70 = 0.
    D = 9 + 280 = 289 = 17^2  →  x = (−3 + 17) / 2 = 7 (reject x = −10).
    Asha is 7 years old.

The discipline of always rejecting roots that contradict the physical situation is as important as the algebra itself. Negative breadths, negative speeds and negative ages are common formal solutions that must be discarded.


SUMMARY

- A quadratic equation in one variable x is one that can be written as a x^2 + b x + c = 0 with a ≠ 0.
- A quadratic equation has at most two real roots; geometrically these are the x-intercepts of the parabola y = a x^2 + b x + c.
- Three solving methods: (i) factorisation by splitting the middle term, (ii) completing the square, (iii) the quadratic formula x = (−b ± √(b^2 − 4 a c)) / (2 a).
- The discriminant D = b^2 − 4 a c controls the nature of the roots: D > 0 → two distinct real roots, D = 0 → one repeated real root, D < 0 → no real roots.
- For roots α, β of a x^2 + b x + c = 0: α + β = −b / a and α β = c / a. From given roots p, q the equation is x^2 − (p + q) x + p q = 0.
- Word problems are solved in four steps: name the unknown, build the quadratic, solve it, discard roots that contradict the context.
