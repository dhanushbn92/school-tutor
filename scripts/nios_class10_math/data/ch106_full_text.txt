CHAPTER 8 — PERCENTAGE AND ITS APPLICATIONS

Introduction

The word "percent" comes from the Latin "per centum" — meaning "per hundred". A percentage is just a fraction with denominator 100. The notation 25 % means 25 per 100, or 25/100 = 0.25. Because "out of 100" is a universal yardstick, percentages let us compare quantities of very different sizes and read off ratios at a glance: a 60 % score on a 50-mark test, a 12 % rise in price, a 40 % discount, all carry the same meaning regardless of the underlying numbers.

This chapter develops percentage as a language and applies it to the workhorse problems of commercial mathematics: increase / decrease, profit and loss, discount, and simple and compound interest. Each application has the same backbone — a base, a rate (in %) and a result — so once you have the percentage idea, every section is a variation of the same theme.


8.1 PERCENTAGE — DEFINITION AND CONVERSIONS

A percent is a fraction with denominator 100. The percentage sign % is shorthand for "per 100":
    25 % = 25/100 = 1/4 = 0.25.

Three forms of the same number. Every percentage corresponds to a decimal and to a fraction in lowest terms. Converting between them is mechanical:
- Percent → decimal: divide by 100. 7.5 % = 0.075.
- Percent → fraction: write over 100, then simplify. 60 % = 60/100 = 3/5.
- Decimal → percent: multiply by 100. 0.4 = 40 %.
- Fraction → percent: convert to a fraction over 100 (or compute the decimal × 100). 1/8 = 12.5 %; 5/4 = 125 %.

Percentages can exceed 100 % (when the part is larger than the whole — common with growth rates) and can be less than 0 % only as a notational convenience for losses.

Percentage of a quantity. "x % of A" means (x/100) · A.
- 20 % of 250 = (20/100) · 250 = 50.
- 12.5 % of 80 = 0.125 · 80 = 10.

Expressing one quantity as a percentage of another. "A as a percentage of B" is (A / B) · 100 %.
- 18 as a percentage of 50 = (18/50) · 100 % = 36 %.
- 7.5 as a percentage of 25 = (7.5/25) · 100 % = 30 %.


8.2 INCREASE AND DECREASE PERCENT

When a quantity changes from an OLD value to a NEW value, the absolute change is (NEW − OLD); the percentage change is (NEW − OLD)/OLD · 100 %.
- A positive percentage change is an INCREASE.
- A negative percentage change is a DECREASE.

Examples.
- Price rises from ₹400 to ₹460. Change = 60; % change = (60/400) · 100 % = 15 % increase.
- Population falls from 5000 to 4500. Change = −500; % change = (−500/5000) · 100 % = −10 % (i.e. a 10 % decrease).

Reverse direction — given the rate, find the new value.
- If A increases by p %, the new value is A · (1 + p/100).
- If A decreases by p %, the new value is A · (1 − p/100).

A 20 % increase on ₹500 → ₹500 · 1.20 = ₹600. A 15 % decrease on ₹500 → ₹500 · 0.85 = ₹425.

Successive percentages. A 10 % increase followed by a 10 % decrease does NOT cancel out:
    A · 1.10 · 0.90 = 0.99 A,
which is a 1 % NET decrease. Two successive p % and q % changes give an overall change of (p + q + p·q/100) % — derive this once and remember.


8.3 PROFIT AND LOSS

In commerce, the COST PRICE (CP) is the price at which a trader buys an item; the SELLING PRICE (SP) is the price at which the trader sells.
- If SP > CP: the trader makes a PROFIT (gain). Profit = SP − CP.
- If SP < CP: the trader makes a LOSS. Loss = CP − SP.
- Profit % = (Profit / CP) · 100 %.
- Loss % = (Loss / CP) · 100 %.

Note: profit / loss percentages are ALWAYS computed on the cost price (CP), not on the selling price.

Going both ways.
- Given CP and gain %: SP = CP · (1 + gain%/100).
- Given CP and loss %: SP = CP · (1 − loss%/100).
- Given SP and gain %: CP = SP / (1 + gain%/100).
- Given SP and loss %: CP = SP / (1 − loss%/100).

Examples.
- CP = ₹500, gain 12 %. SP = 500 · 1.12 = ₹560.
- CP = ₹500, loss 8 %. SP = 500 · 0.92 = ₹460.
- A radio is sold for ₹540 at a gain of 8 %. Find CP. CP = 540 / 1.08 = ₹500.

Gain or loss on a transaction with multiple items: lump the totals — total CP and total SP — then apply the formula.


8.4 DISCOUNT

Discount is a reduction on the MARKED PRICE (MP). The shopkeeper marks an item at MP and offers a discount d % off; the customer pays the SELLING PRICE (SP).
    SP = MP · (1 − d/100).

Note. Discount is on MP, not on CP. The shopkeeper still has to make a profit (or at least cover cost) — the discount is part of marketing, not a charity.

Combined operations. A typical "back-of-the-book" problem: 'A shopkeeper marks his goods 25 % above CP and gives a 20 % discount. Find his profit %.'
    Let CP = ₹100. MP = 125. SP after discount = 125 · 0.80 = 100.
    Profit = 0; profit % = 0.

So a 25 % markup followed by 20 % discount is a no-profit transaction. The shopkeeper here breaks even.


8.5 SIMPLE INTEREST

When you borrow money (the PRINCIPAL P) at an interest RATE r % per annum for TIME t years, the SIMPLE INTEREST you owe is
    SI = (P · r · t) / 100.

The AMOUNT you repay is A = P + SI = P(1 + r·t/100).

Examples.
- P = ₹2000, r = 8 % p.a., t = 3 years. SI = 2000 · 8 · 3 / 100 = ₹480. A = 2480.
- P = ₹5000, r = 12 % p.a., t = 1.5 years. SI = 5000 · 12 · 1.5 / 100 = ₹900. A = 5900.

Simple interest is "simple" because the interest itself does NOT earn interest. Each year you pay r % of the original principal — flat.


8.6 COMPOUND INTEREST

In compound interest the interest earned at the end of each period is ADDED to the principal, so future interest is computed on a larger base. The principal grows.

Yearly compounding. After t years, the AMOUNT is
    A = P · (1 + r/100)^t.
The compound interest is CI = A − P.

Example.
- P = ₹2000, r = 10 % p.a., t = 3 years.
  A = 2000 · (1.10)^3 = 2000 · 1.331 = ₹2662.
  CI = 2662 − 2000 = ₹662.
  By comparison SI for the same data = 2000 · 10 · 3 / 100 = ₹600. CI − SI = ₹62.

The CI is always at least the SI (and strictly greater when t > 1 year and r > 0).

Compounding at other intervals. If the rate r % is per annum but the interest is compounded n times a year, then in t years the amount is
    A = P · (1 + r/(100·n))^(n·t).
- Half-yearly: n = 2.
- Quarterly: n = 4.
- Monthly: n = 12.

The same ₹2000 at 10 % p.a. compounded HALF-YEARLY for 3 years:
    A = 2000 · (1 + 5/100)^6 = 2000 · 1.05^6 ≈ 2000 · 1.3401 ≈ ₹2680.
Compounding more frequently gives a slightly larger amount.

Difference between CI and SI for 2 years (yearly compounding). A common shortcut:
    CI − SI (for 2 years) = P · (r/100)^2.
Quick check: P = 2000, r = 10 %. CI − SI = 2000 · (0.1)^2 = 20. (For 2 years; the 3-year answer above was 62.)


SUMMARY

- A percentage is a fraction with denominator 100; convert freely between fractions, decimals and percentages.
- "x % of A" = (x/100) · A; "A as a % of B" = (A/B) · 100 %.
- Percentage change = (NEW − OLD)/OLD · 100 %. Use 1 ± p/100 to get the new value directly.
- In commerce, profit / loss % is ALWAYS computed on CP.
- Discount is computed on the MARKED PRICE.
- Simple interest: SI = Prt/100; amount = P(1 + rt/100).
- Compound interest: A = P(1 + r/100)^t for yearly compounding; replace by A = P(1 + r/(100n))^(nt) for n compounding periods per year.
- For 2-year yearly compounding, the CI − SI shortcut is P(r/100)^2.
