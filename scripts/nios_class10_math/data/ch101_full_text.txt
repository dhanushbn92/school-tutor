CHAPTER 3 — ALGEBRAIC EXPRESSIONS AND POLYNOMIALS

Introduction

In Chapters 1 and 2 we worked with numbers — integers, rational numbers, exponents, radicals. Algebra extends this to letters that stand for numbers. Once we treat numbers as variables, we can write rules like "the perimeter of a square of side a is 4a" once and apply them to every square. The whole machinery of algebra rests on the idea of an expression — a rule built out of numbers and variables — and on the special class of expressions called polynomials.

This chapter develops the vocabulary of algebraic expressions, classifies them, defines polynomials and their degrees, and shows how to add, subtract and multiply them. The next chapter (Special Products and Factorisation) will use these tools heavily.


3.1 ALGEBRAIC EXPRESSIONS — VOCABULARY

A variable is a symbol (usually a letter such as x, y, a, n) that stands for a number. A constant is a fixed number such as 3, −7, 2/5 or √2.

An algebraic expression is built up from constants and variables using the four basic operations (+, −, ×, ÷) and powers. Some examples:
    3x + 5,    2a + 3b − 7,    x^2 − 4x + 4,    5p^3 − 2p^2 + p − 9.

A term is a part of an algebraic expression that is separated from other parts by a + or − sign. For example, 5p^3 − 2p^2 + p − 9 has four terms: 5p^3, −2p^2, p and −9. The sign immediately before a term belongs to it.

Each term is a product of a constant part (the coefficient) and a variable part (one or more variables raised to non-negative integer powers). In 5p^3:
    coefficient = 5;
    variable part = p^3.
In the term p (i.e. 1·p), the coefficient is 1 — it's just not written. In −x^2, the coefficient is −1.

A like term has the same variable part. So 7x^2 and −4x^2 are like terms (both x^2), but 7x^2 and 7x are unlike (different powers of x). Like terms can be combined by adding or subtracting their coefficients:
    7x^2 + (−4x^2) = 3x^2.
Unlike terms cannot be combined this way; they remain as separate terms in the simplified expression.

A constant term is a term with no variable part — just a number, e.g. the −9 in 5p^3 − 2p^2 + p − 9.


3.2 CLASSIFYING EXPRESSIONS BY NUMBER OF TERMS

Algebraic expressions are commonly named by their number of terms after combining like terms.
- A monomial is an expression with exactly one term: 5x, −7p^3, 2/3.
- A binomial has two unlike terms: 3x + 5, p^2 − 7p.
- A trinomial has three unlike terms: x^2 + 3x − 2.

Expressions with more than three terms are simply called multinomials (or polynomials, when the powers are non-negative integers — see §3.3).


3.3 POLYNOMIALS

A polynomial is an algebraic expression in which:
1. The variables appear only with non-negative integer powers (1, 2, 3, …, or 0, which gives a constant).
2. There is no division by any variable.

Examples of polynomials:
    7,                   (degree 0, a constant polynomial)
    3x + 5,              (degree 1, linear)
    x^2 − 4x + 4,        (degree 2, quadratic)
    2y^3 − y + 6,        (degree 3, cubic)
    5z^4 + z^2 − 1.      (degree 4, biquadratic)

Examples that are NOT polynomials:
    3/x  (variable in the denominator),
    √x  (fractional power 1/2),
    x^(−2) + 5  (negative power),
    2/(x + 1)  (variable in the denominator).

Degree of a polynomial in one variable. The degree is the largest exponent of the variable. So x^2 − 4x + 4 has degree 2; 5z^4 + z^2 − 1 has degree 4. By convention, the zero polynomial 0 has no degree (or degree undefined).

Degree of a polynomial in more than one variable. Compute the degree of each term as the SUM of the exponents of all variables in that term, and take the maximum.
- For 3x^2 y, the term degree is 2 + 1 = 3.
- For 5x y^2 z, the term degree is 1 + 2 + 1 = 4.
- For 4xy + 7x^2 y^3, the degrees are 1 + 1 = 2 and 2 + 3 = 5; so the polynomial degree is 5.

Special vocabulary by degree:
- Degree 0: constant polynomial.
- Degree 1: linear polynomial.
- Degree 2: quadratic polynomial.
- Degree 3: cubic polynomial.
- Degree 4: biquadratic (or quartic) polynomial.

Standard form. A polynomial in one variable is in standard form when its terms are written in descending order of degree:
    5z^4 + z^2 − 1     (standard form),
    not  z^2 + 5z^4 − 1.
Standard form makes it easy to read off the degree, the leading coefficient (the coefficient of the highest-degree term — here 5) and the constant term.


3.4 ADDING AND SUBTRACTING POLYNOMIALS

To add or subtract two polynomials, combine their LIKE terms. Two methods work; pick whichever is clearer.

Horizontal method. Write both polynomials side by side, group like terms, then combine.
Example: add (3x^2 − 5x + 2) and (x^2 + 7x − 6).
    (3x^2 − 5x + 2) + (x^2 + 7x − 6)
  = (3x^2 + x^2) + (−5x + 7x) + (2 − 6)
  = 4x^2 + 2x − 4.

Vertical method. Stack the polynomials so that like terms align in columns, then add column by column. Useful when the polynomials are long.
    3x^2  − 5x  + 2
  +  x^2  + 7x  − 6
    ─────────────────
    4x^2  + 2x  − 4

Subtraction. To subtract, take the additive inverse of every term in the polynomial being subtracted, then add. Equivalently: change the sign of every term you're subtracting, and add.
Example: subtract (x^2 + 7x − 6) from (3x^2 − 5x + 2).
    (3x^2 − 5x + 2) − (x^2 + 7x − 6)
  = (3x^2 − 5x + 2) + (−x^2 − 7x + 6)
  = (3x^2 − x^2) + (−5x − 7x) + (2 + 6)
  = 2x^2 − 12x + 8.

The result of adding or subtracting two polynomials is itself a polynomial; its degree is at most the larger of the two original degrees (and can be smaller if leading terms cancel).


3.5 MULTIPLYING POLYNOMIALS

The cornerstone is the distributive law:
    a(b + c) = ab + ac,
    (a + b)(c + d) = ac + ad + bc + bd.
For polynomials in general, every term of the first is multiplied by every term of the second, and the results are added.

Multiplying a monomial and a polynomial. Distribute over each term.
Example: 3x^2 · (2x + 5) = (3x^2)(2x) + (3x^2)(5) = 6x^3 + 15x^2.

Multiplying two binomials. Apply distribution four times — often remembered as FOIL: First, Outer, Inner, Last.
Example: (x + 2)(x + 3)
  = x · x + x · 3 + 2 · x + 2 · 3
  = x^2 + 3x + 2x + 6
  = x^2 + 5x + 6.
The middle terms 3x and 2x are like terms; combining them gives 5x.

Multiplying two general polynomials. The vertical (long-multiplication) method is reliable for long polynomials.
Example: (2x^2 − 3x + 1) · (x − 4).
    2x^2  − 3x  + 1
  ×                    x  − 4
    ──────────────────────────
    2x^3  − 3x^2 +  x         (× x)
  −8x^2  +12x  − 4             (× −4)
    ──────────────────────────
    2x^3  −11x^2 +13x − 4.

The degree of the product is the SUM of the degrees of the two polynomials.

Sign rules carry over from arithmetic with signed numbers: positive × positive = positive; negative × negative = positive; positive × negative = negative.


3.6 LAWS OF EXPONENTS IN POLYNOMIAL MULTIPLICATION

Polynomial multiplication uses the laws of exponents you saw in Chapter 2. The most important ones in this chapter are:
    x^a · x^b = x^(a+b),         (product law)
    (x^a)^b   = x^(a·b),         (power-of-a-power)
    (xy)^n    = x^n · y^n.       (power-of-a-product)

These let you multiply terms like (3x^2 y) · (5x y^4) cleanly:
    coefficients: 3 · 5 = 15;
    x parts: x^2 · x = x^3 (using x^a · x^b = x^(a+b));
    y parts: y · y^4 = y^5;
so the product is 15 x^3 y^5.


3.7 VALUE OF A POLYNOMIAL AT A NUMBER

Substituting a specific number for the variable converts a polynomial into a number — its value at that point.
Example: evaluate p(x) = x^2 − 4x + 4 at x = 3.
    p(3) = 3^2 − 4 · 3 + 4 = 9 − 12 + 4 = 1.
Evaluation is the bridge from algebra back to arithmetic. We'll use it heavily when we look for zeros (roots) of polynomials in later chapters.

A zero (or root) of a polynomial p(x) is a value of x for which p(x) = 0. For p(x) = x^2 − 4x + 4, p(2) = 4 − 8 + 4 = 0, so x = 2 is a zero. Quadratics like this — and all polynomials of degree n — have at most n zeros (allowing repeats). The detailed study of zeros is the subject of the chapters on Linear and Quadratic Equations later in this module.


SUMMARY

- Variables stand for numbers; constants are fixed numbers; algebraic expressions are built from them with +, −, ×, ÷ and powers.
- A term is a part of an expression separated by + or −. Each term has a coefficient (numerical part) and a variable part.
- Like terms have the same variable part and combine by adding/subtracting coefficients.
- Polynomials are algebraic expressions whose variables have non-negative integer powers and no variable in any denominator.
- Degree of a polynomial in one variable: the largest exponent. In more than one variable: the largest sum-of-exponents across the terms.
- Standard form: terms in descending order of degree.
- Add / subtract polynomials by combining like terms (horizontal or vertical layout).
- Multiply polynomials by distributing every term of one across every term of the other; degree of product = sum of degrees.
- Evaluating a polynomial at a number means substituting the number for the variable and arithmetic-ing. A value at which the polynomial equals zero is a zero of the polynomial.
