Manure vs Chemical Fertilizer

Both add plant nutrients, but they behave very differently in the soil.

Manure (organic)

  • Made from decomposed plant/animal waste (compost, cow dung, green manure).
  • Adds humus - improves soil texture, water holding and aeration.
  • Nutrients released slowly; feeds soil micro-organisms.
  • Cheap, eco-friendly, low nutrient concentration.

Chemical fertilizer (inorganic)

  • Factory-made salts: urea (N), superphosphate (P), muriate of potash (K).
  • High in one specific nutrient; acts fast, boosts yield quickly.
  • Adds no humus; overuse harms soil and pollutes water (eutrophication).
  • Costly; must be applied in correct dose.

Crop Rotation

Growing different crops in a field in a planned sequence keeps soil fertile. Legumes (pulses) fix nitrogen through root nodule bacteria (Rhizobium), so cereals grown next need less fertilizer.

Animal Husbandry

The science of rearing and breeding animals for food and useful products. Tap a card for details on improving yield and breeds.

Improving Yield & Breeds

Common methods across all animal farming.

Better breeds

Cross-breeding local (hardy, disease-resistant) with exotic (high-yield) varieties - e.g. cattle breeds, layer/broiler poultry.

Feed & nutrition

Balanced, nutritious feed and clean water raise milk, egg, meat and honey output.

Health & shelter

Vaccination, disease control, hygienic ventilated housing reduce losses.

Scientific care

Proper breeding records, artificial insemination and record-keeping improve stock over generations.

Green Revolution: Yield Concept

Pros & Cons of High-Yield Methods

Advantages

  • Much higher food-grain production; helped India reach self-sufficiency.
  • More food per hectare from the same land.
  • Boosted farm incomes and food security.

Concerns

  • Overuse of chemical fertilizers & pesticides degrades soil and pollutes water.
  • Heavy irrigation lowers water table; loss of crop diversity.
  • High input cost; benefits uneven for small farmers.

Sustainable farming aims for high yield with low harm: organic manure, crop rotation, drip irrigation and integrated pest management.

Pest Management

Two broad approaches to protecting crops from pests.

Biological control

  • Uses natural enemies - ladybird beetles eat aphids, Trichogramma wasps.
  • Bio-pesticides like Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) and neem.
  • Eco-friendly, safe for soil and no chemical residue.

Chemical control

  • Synthetic pesticides/insecticides kill pests fast.
  • Effective but residues harm health and helpful insects.
  • Overuse causes pest resistance and pollution.

Manure vs fertilizer

Manure = natural, slow, adds humus, improves soil. Fertilizer = manufactured, fast, nutrient-rich but adds no humus and can pollute if overused.

Crop rotation

Planned sequence of crops (especially legumes) that restores soil fertility, controls pests and weeds, and avoids nutrient exhaustion.

Irrigation

Supplying water to crops - canals, wells, tube-wells, drip and sprinkler systems. Drip irrigation saves the most water.

Animal husbandry terms

Poultry = birds for eggs/meat; Pisciculture = fish farming; Apiculture = bee-keeping for honey/wax; Dairy = milk from cattle.

Green Revolution

Rapid rise in food-grain output using HYV seeds, irrigation, fertilizers & pesticides. Made India self-sufficient in food grains.

Storage

Protects harvested grain from moisture, insects, rodents and fungi. Grains are dried and kept in silos/granaries; fumigants control pests.

Nitrogen fixing

Rhizobium bacteria in legume root nodules convert atmospheric nitrogen into a usable form, naturally enriching the soil.
