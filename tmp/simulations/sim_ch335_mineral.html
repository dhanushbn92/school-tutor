Match the symptom

Mobility matters: deficiency of mobile elements (N, P, K, Mg) shows first in older leaves, because the plant retranslocates them to young growing tissue. Immobile elements (Ca, Fe, S, B, Cu) show symptoms first in young leaves and growing tips.

Uptake & the Criteria of Essentiality

How do roots take up mineral ions, and how do scientists decide an element is truly "essential"?

How minerals are absorbed

Passive absorption

Ions move down their electrochemical gradient into root epidermis/cortex without direct ATP use — by diffusion, ion channels and mass flow in water. Fast, non-selective, no metabolic energy.

Active absorption

Ions are pumped against the gradient using energy from ATP (via H+-ATPase and carrier proteins). Selective, saturable, needs respiration — blocked by low O2 or metabolic poisons.

The Casparian strip in the endodermis blocks the apoplast pathway, forcing ions through the symplast — letting the root select which ions reach the xylem for transport to the shoot.

Criteria of essentiality (Arnon & Stout)

  • Necessity: the plant cannot complete its life cycle (grow, flower, set seed) without the element.
  • Specificity: the requirement is specific — no other element can fully replace it.
  • Direct role: the element is directly involved in metabolism (a structural part of a molecule or an enzyme activator), not just correcting soil chemistry.

Hydroponics

Growing plants in a soil-free, aerated nutrient solution of known composition. By leaving out one element at a time, researchers identified which elements are essential and what deficiency each causes — this is how the essential-element list was built and how deficiency symptoms were catalogued.

Functional roles at a glance: N & S build amino acids/proteins; P builds ATP, nucleic acids & membranes; K activates enzymes & controls stomata/osmosis; Ca is a middle-lamella (pectate) and signalling component; Mg is the central atom of chlorophyll; Fe, Cu, Mo are cofactors in electron transport & nitrogen metabolism.
