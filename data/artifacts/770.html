Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation

F = G · m₁m₂ / r²

Every pair of masses in the universe attracts each other. The force F grows with both masses (m₁, m₂) and shrinks with the square of the distance r — double the distance and the force drops to a quarter.

G6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg² — the universal gravitational constant, same everywhere.
g = GM/R²On a planet's surface, gravity gives every object the same acceleration g, whatever its mass.
Free fallv = gt and s = ½gt² (starting from rest) — used live in the Free Fall tab.
OrbitsGravity supplies the centripetal force: GMm/r² = mv²/r, so v꜀ = √(GM/r) and vₑ = √2·v꜀.

Quick check — 3 questions

1. If the distance between two masses is doubled, the gravitational force between them becomes…

F ∝ 1/r². Doubling r makes r² four times bigger, so the force falls to ¼. This "inverse-square law" is why gravity weakens quickly with distance.

2. A hammer and a feather are dropped together on the Moon (no air). What happens?

Same time! Acceleration in free fall (g) does not depend on the falling object's mass — g = GM/R² contains only the Moon's mass. Astronaut David Scott actually did this experiment on Apollo 15. On Earth, only air resistance slows the feather.

3. A satellite stays in a circular orbit because…

It is falling — and missing. Gravity still acts strongly in orbit (that's the centripetal force GMm/r² = mv²/r). The satellite moves sideways so fast that as it falls, the Earth's surface curves away beneath it. Try this in the Orbit tab: 100% speed = circle.
