Driving forces

Sap ascent speed  1.0 relative
Water potential (Ψ) drives osmosis: soil Ψ is higher than root-cell Ψ, so water flows in. Transpiration pull creates the most negative Ψ at the leaves — the main force lifting water in tall plants.

Environmental factors

Transpiration rate  1.0 relative
High light & temperature and wind raise the rate; high humidity lowers it. Closed stomata (e.g. at night or in drought) nearly stop water loss.

Translocation in Phloem (Pressure–Flow)

Sugar made in leaves (source) is loaded into phloem, drawing in water by osmosis; the pressure pushes sap toward growing or storage regions (sink).

Mass-flow controls

Phloem flow  1.0 relative
Xylem carries water & minerals upward only (dead, hollow vessels). Phloem carries sugars from source to sink — up or down (living sieve tubes with companion cells). Switch the source to a storage root to reverse the flow.

Key Facts

  • Osmosis: net movement of water across a semipermeable membrane from higher to lower water potential; root hairs absorb soil water this way.
  • Water potential (Ψ): the free energy of water; pure water = 0, solutions are negative. Water moves from high Ψ to low Ψ.
  • Cohesion–tension: water molecules stick together (cohesion) and to xylem walls (adhesion); transpiration pull lifts an unbroken water column up the xylem.
  • Root pressure: a positive push from the roots (best seen as guttation at night); it aids ascent but cannot alone raise water in tall trees.
  • Stomata: pores flanked by guard cells; they open when turgid (K⁺ and water enter) and close when flaccid, regulating gas exchange and water loss.
  • Translocation: transport of food (sucrose) in phloem by the pressure-flow (mass-flow) mechanism, from source to sink, bidirectionally.
