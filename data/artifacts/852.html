Biodiversity Hotspots of India

A hotspot is a biologically rich region under serious threat. India has four.

India's four biodiversity hotspots: the Himalaya, the Western Ghats & Sri Lanka, the Indo-Burma region (north-east India), and Sundaland (Nicobar Islands). They hold exceptional endemism but face habitat loss.

⚠ Threats

  • Deforestation — clearing forests for farming, mining, urbanisation.
  • Overexploitation — harvesting faster than resources can renew.
  • Habitat loss & fragmentation — the leading cause of extinction.
  • Poaching — illegal hunting for skin, horn, ivory, tusks.
  • Pollution & invasive species add further pressure.

✔ Solutions

  • Afforestation & reforestation to rebuild green cover.
  • Wildlife protection laws — Wildlife (Protection) Act 1972; Forest (Conservation) Act 1980.
  • The 3Rs — Reduce, Reuse, Recycle.
  • Sustainable development — meeting present needs without compromising future generations.
  • Protected areas & community participation (e.g. Chipko movement, JFM).

💡 Key Facts

  • In-situ conservation protects species in their natural habitat: national parks, wildlife sanctuaries and biosphere reserves.
  • Ex-situ conservation protects species outside their habitat: zoos, botanical gardens, seed banks, gene banks and cryopreservation.
  • India has 18 biosphere reserves and four global biodiversity hotspots.
  • Project Tiger began in 1973; Project Elephant began in 1992.
  • Sustainable development = using resources to meet present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet theirs (Brundtland, 1987).
